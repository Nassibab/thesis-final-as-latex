\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
 
Generative AI allows people to build software by describing what they
want in plain language. Employees without programming training can now
create working prototypes within hours or days \parencite{kobiella2026}.
Many of these prototypes, however, never become software that an
organisation can safely rely on. \textcite{maes2026} calls this a
deployment paradox. The difficult part is no longer writing code. It is
making sure that the software is secure, legally compliant, reliable,
useful for the business and clearly owned \parencite{farrag2026,kobiella2026}.
Without guidance, applications built outside IT can become shadow IT
and technical debt that the organisation later has to maintain
\parencite{viljoen2024}.
 
This thesis addresses this problem in the HR department of SIXT. Since
June 2026, the researcher and the researcher's manager have built three
HR applications with generative AI. Their experience, together with the
literature, forms the basis of a framework that guides non-technical
employees from an idea to software that can be used in daily work.
 
 
\subsection{Research Objectives and Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}
 
The thesis answers one main research question:
 
\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ:} How can a governance and development framework enable
non-technical employees to develop software with generative AI that is
ready for production use in a large organisation?
\end{quote}
 
The objective of this thesis is to develop and evaluate a governance and
development framework for software that non-technical employees create
with generative AI. The framework should help them move from a prototype
to production use, keep risks under control and involve experts where
needed.

Four sub-questions divide the main question into smaller parts:
 
\begin{description}
    \item[SQ1] Which difficulties arise when non-technical employees use
    generative AI to build software for production use, and where do
    they need support?
    \item[SQ2] Which working practices, safeguards and organisational
    conditions should a framework include to address these difficulties?
    \item[SQ3] Does the framework address these difficulties in
    practice, and how useful and feasible do practitioners consider it?
    \item[SQ4] Which organisational conditions does the framework
    require, and what limits its transfer to other settings?
\end{description}
 
The study answers these questions in three phases. The first phase
examines the starting point with the first application and answers
SQ1 (Section~\ref{sec:starting-point}). The second phase develops the
framework and answers SQ2 (Section~\ref{sec:framework}). The third phase
evaluates the framework with the third application and practitioner
interviews and answers SQ3 and SQ4 (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).
 

\subsection{Scope and Delimitations}
\label{sec:scope}

This thesis examines software that is developed primarily by
non-technical employees who use generative AI coding tools, such as
Claude Code, as their main means of development. The defining
characteristic is therefore the way in which the software is created,
not whether the resulting application itself contains AI functionality.
Following this focus, non-technical employees, referred to here as
citizen developers, are defined as employees who neither hold a
professional software engineering role nor have formal training in
software development, although they may possess general digital
skills. [Add one sentence on how the development team fits this
definition, e.g. that the researcher studies Business Information
Systems but had no professional programming experience before the
study.]

The study is situated in the HR department of SIXT. The applications
examined are internal tools that display, edit or track HR data for a
limited group of internal users. Their development was supported by
HR IT and, where necessary, by central IT, within the resources these
units could provide.

Several related areas lie outside the scope of this thesis. Traditional
low-code and no-code platforms, which do not rely on AI code
generation, are considered only for comparison. Likewise, the thesis
does not address professional software engineers who use AI to
accelerate their own work, since its focus lies on employees who
depend on expert support. Risks arising from AI features within an
application, such as harmful chatbot responses, are also excluded, as
these are addressed by dedicated methods such as MeRGE
\parencite{fetzer2026}. Furthermore, the framework does not replace
formal legal, security or data protection assessments; instead, it
identifies the points at which such specialist expertise is required.
Finally, business value is considered only as part of the first gate
of the framework, and no separate model for its calculation is
developed.

Importantly, the framework does not presuppose that every idea should
reach production. Stopping an idea, restricting its scope or handing it
over to IT are regarded as legitimate outcomes, since preventing
unsuitable applications from advancing is itself part of responsible
governance.

\subsection{Research Design}
\label{sec:method-design}
 
The study follows a design science research (DSR) approach. DSR creates
and evaluates artifacts that solve practical problems. In this thesis,
the artifact is the governance and development framework. Following
\textcite{sein2011adr}, it is an \emph{ensemble artifact}: it combines
technical elements, such as approved tools and sandboxes, with
organisational elements, such as roles, gates and working practices.

DSR fits this thesis because its aim is not only to describe a problem
but to develop a usable solution and assess it under real
organisational constraints. The literature provides foundations for the
design. It cannot show on its own, however, whether citizen developers
can apply the framework with limited IT support. This requires evidence
from the setting in which the framework is meant to be used.

\textcite{peffers2007dsrm} describe DSR as a process of problem
identification, definition of solution objectives, design and
development, demonstration, evaluation and communication. The study is
also informed by action design research (ADR), which treats building an
artifact, intervening in an organisation and evaluating the artifact as
interwoven activities rather than separate steps \parencite{sein2011adr}.
\textcite{sein2011adr} criticise sequential process models such as that
of \textcite{peffers2007dsrm} for separating building from evaluation.
This critique applies here: the framework was developed while software
was being built at SIXT, and practical experience changed it along the
way. The process of \textcite{peffers2007dsrm} is therefore used to
structure how the research is reported, not to claim that all
activities happened in a strict order.

ADR served as methodological guidance; the study did not follow every
stage and principle of a formal ADR process. The study is instead
reported in three phases (Table~\ref{tab:phases}). These phases are not
complete research cycles. Phase~1 assesses the starting point before
any version of the framework existed. Phase~2 partly applies an earlier
version of the framework and revises it.
Phase~3 applies and evaluates the final version, but its findings are
not built into a further version within this thesis.

The evaluation within the thesis was both formative and summative
\parencite{sein2011adr,venable2016feds}. Formative evaluation
takes place while an artifact is still being developed and aims to
improve it. Summative evaluation takes place once the artifact
is finished and aims to judge its value. In Phase~2, experience and
feedback from applying an earlier version shaped the framework
(formative evaluation). In Phase~3, the final version is evaluated as a
whole (summative evaluation). The evaluation section documents the summative evaluation, while the formative evaluation is reported less systematically at several points in this thesis. A controlled experiment was not
feasible. Such an experiment would compare groups with and without the
framework under identical conditions, so that any difference could be
attributed to the framework alone. \textcite{sein2011adr} argue that,
in organisational design research, an authentic setting, meaning
real use in a real organisation, matters more than a controlled one.
The three applications therefore offer a natural comparison:
one application was built without the framework, one with parts of it
and one with the final version. This comparison is uncontrolled,
because other factors, such as the team's growing experience or the quality of the organisational adn technical enviroment or the coding models, also
changed between the applications.


The design decisions of the framework drew on several sources: the
researcher's own experience while building the applications, feedback
from users and citizen developers, scientific literature and
practitioner sources. Practitioner sources were adopted when they
proved useful in practice and were perceived positively in informal
feedback from citizen developers. The exact origin of each design
decision was not recorded. Section~\ref{sec:framework} therefore
presents the final combination of decisions, together with the
practical arguments for each and the scientific or practitioner
sources behind it.


\subsection{Research Context, Roles and Artifact}
\label{sec:method-context}

The study takes place within SIXT HR. The researcher and their manager acted as citizen developers. Both began without professional programming skills and acquired knowledge through practical development, training and support from IT. The researcher held two roles: designer of the framework and citizen developer. The manager acted occasionally as a co-citizen developer and mainly as the business owner. HR IT and, occasionally, central IT provided support within their available resources. The existing corporate environment evolved throughout the process as requested by the researcher, although certain limitations remained. It will be explained in more detail later on. Therefore, the thesis distinguishes this existing infrastructure from the practices it proposes.

Three HR applications provide the practical setting for the study.
They address different business tasks but share a focus on making
structured HR information easier to view, update and track.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Organisation explorer:} The organisation explorer presents organisational structures and
related HR information through a visual interface. It enables users
to explore the organisation and access information according to
their assigned roles. The application combines data presentation
with dashboard functionality to make organisational information
easier to navigate. It was the team's first application and was
developed without the thesis framework. User feedback revealed that
some interface choices and features were unnecessarily complicated,
leading to substantial rework.

\textbf{Reorganisation tool:} The reorganisation tool supports working with organisational data
in the context of structural changes. It provides a visual interface
for reviewing information, making changes and tracking relevant
data. Different access roles determine which functions and
information users can access. The application also includes a
chatbot that helps users obtain information. With users' consent,
chatbot conversations were reviewed to identify incorrect responses
and opportunities for improvement. Compared with the first
application, development included an initial prototype and checks
for existing libraries before implementing functionality.

\textbf{HR job-profile tool:} The HR job-profile tool supports creating, viewing and maintaining
structured job-profile information. It provides an interface for
working with the underlying HR data, with access determined by
user roles. The application serves as the principal case for
applying the combined framework, including its enabling environment
and working practices. Development incorporated training, Git
version control, prototyping, library checks and development records
such as \texttt{plan.md}. The evaluation examines the actual coverage
of these practices and the exceptions encountered during their use.
\end{quote}

Across the three applications, the main functions involve visualising,
editing and tracking HR data. Their different business purposes
provide opportunities to examine development challenges across
related use cases within the same organisational setting.

The organisational environment
included approved tools, sandboxing, deployment infrastructure,
password management and processes for requesting data and
technical assistance. Their availability and application varied
during the study. These organisational and technical conditions
are described in the framework chapter.

The framework constitutes the research artifact and consists of
two connected parts. The enabling environment describes the
organisational and technical conditions needed to support citizen
development. The working practices describe development activities,
responsibilities, evidence requirements, practices for AI-assisted
development and decision gates. Existing organisational enviroment and technical infrastructure
is distinguished from practices proposed or adapted by the thesis.

The study period covers approximately June to November 2026.
During this period, the framework developed through informal
iterations. Two main versions are distinguished. The first focused
primarily on working practices. The second retained most of these
practices and added the organisational and technical conditions
needed to apply them. The second version is presented as the final
artifact and forms the basis of the final practitioner evaluation.
Earlier development experiences and formative discussions informed
its design. All three applications are in operational use at SIXT.

 
\subsection{Research Objectives and Phases}
\label{sec:method-phases}
 
The study has two parts. The first examines the starting point for
AI-assisted citizen development at SIXT HR. It asks how far two
employees without professional programming experience could take an
application, which difficulties they encountered and where they needed
support. The second part uses these findings to develop a framework and
then evaluates it. The main objective is to assess whether the framework
addresses the difficulties that motivated its design. Three objectives,
one per phase, guide the study.
 
\textbf{1. Establish the starting point and identify difficulties
(Phase~1).} The first application, the organisation explorer (App~1),
shows how development went before the team used the framework. The
analysis examines which HR functions the team delivered, what users
could do with the application and where development became difficult.
User feedback, discarded work, the failure to restore an earlier
working version and requests for IT help show the limits of the team's
initial approach. These observations define the practical problems the
framework has to address.
 
\textbf{2. Develop a framework that responds to these difficulties
(Phase~2).} The difficulties from App~1 are examined together with the
literature and formative discussions with citizen developers, users and
software engineers. These discussions showed which difficulties other
practitioners recognised and which kinds of support seemed feasible at
SIXT. The second application, the reorganisation tool (App~2), was used
to try selected practices, such as building a prototype before
implementation and checking for existing libraries. The resulting
framework combines working practices with the organisational and
technical conditions needed to apply them. Its design rationale links
each practice or safeguard to the problem it is meant to address
(Section~\ref{sec:framework}).
 
\textbf{3. Evaluate the framework and identify improvements
(Phase~3).} The third application, the HR job-profile tool (App~3), is
the main case for evaluating the framework as a whole. The evaluation
examines which practices the development team applied, how they
influenced development and release decisions, and which difficulties
remained. It also shows where technical expertise or organisational
approval was needed and how SIXT could provide it with reasonable
effort. Development records, tests, user feedback and IT reviews show
what happened in practice. Final practitioner interviews assess whether
the framework addresses problems they recognise, whether its
requirements are feasible and what should be changed. The thesis separates changes that were made and tried during
development from ideas that came up later but were not tested. The
latter are presented as suggestions for future work.

\subsection{Application Evidence}
\label{sec:method-application-evidence}
 
The three applications play different roles in the study
(Table~\ref{tab:phases}). App~1 establishes the starting point, App~2
shows the use of selected practices while the framework was taking
shape, and App~3 is the main case for the final framework. For App~3,
the analysis returns to the difficulties identified in App~1 and
examines what happened when the corresponding practice was applied. For
example, the failure to restore an earlier version of App~1 leads to a
specific question for App~3: what evidence shows that version control
supported recovery or review? The evidence comes from six sources.
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IT reviews.} For each application, the development
    team opened an IT ticket and gave software engineers access to the
    code. A discussion of about 30 minutes followed, in which the
    engineers explained strengths, defects and possible improvements.
    Additional discussions addressed specific needs, such as the
    Workday integration and the improvements needed for production use.  
    The reviews were qualitative, without a common rating scale. This means that the engineers gave their feedback in their own words; they did not
    score the applications on a fixed scale. Conclusions are drawn only
    about what a review actually examined. For example, a review of the
    code structure says nothing about security unless security was also
    checked. Accordingly explicit questions were asked regarding  access control, privacy, security or operational
    readiness to acquire evidence that this aspect was actually examined.


    \item \textbf{Development records and tests.} The Git history
    (App~2 partly, App~3), the tests created and run during development
    and, for App~3, the \texttt{plan.md} file, which records tasks,
    decisions and open questions. The value of \texttt{plan.md} for
    governance depends on what a reviewer can trace from it, such as a
    requirement, a decision or an open check. Responsibility for
    accepting a risk or approving a release is assessed only where the
    records name it.
    \item \textbf{Time and cost.} Development periods and approximate
    Claude Code spend in euros, taken from cloude setting in the resaercher's account. The periods are
    calendar time, not working hours: the researcher worked part-time
    as a working student, typically about 20 hours per week, and exact
    hours per application were not recorded.
    \item \textbf{User feedback.} was collected through walkthrough meetings for App 1. Similar meetings were held for Apps 2 and 3, supplemented by in-app feedback from a broader group of approximately 50 users and feature usage tracking. Users provided quantitative star ratings and qualitative feedback on specific problems. Usage tracking showed which functions people used, while feedback helped identify where they struggled. Together, these sources provided indications of whether users found it easier to complete tasks using the applications. These findings are treated as preliminary indications of business value creation, although stronger claims require concrete evidence of benefits.
    \item \textbf{Requests for help.} IT tickets and other requests for
    technical support, showing where the team could not proceed alone.
    \item \textbf{The development team's own accounts} of what worked
    and what did not.
\end{itemize}
 
App~1 was built before the framework existed and without version
control. Its evidence is therefore partly reconstructed after the
fact, from the IT review, user feedback, cost data and the team's
recollection. Records made during development are treated as stronger
evidence than later recollection, and missing evidence is reported as
missing.


\subsection{Practitioner Interviews}
\label{sec:method-interviews}
 
Practitioner input served two purposes at different points in the
study. During Phases~1 and~2, informal formative discussions with two
citizen developers and two software engineers helped the researcher
understand recurring difficulties and refine the emerging framework.
These discussions were not documented in notes; they are used to
explain design choices, not as evaluation evidence. In Phase~3, final
semi-structured interviews provide the evidence for the criterion
practitioner assessment (Table~\ref{tab:eval-criteria}) and contribute
to SQ3 and SQ4. Where the same people took part in both, their earlier
suggestions are treated as design input and their later judgements as
feedback on the version presented.

Eight experienced practitioners were interviewed. They were three
software engineers (E1--E3) and five citizen developers (C1--C5). They
were selected because of their relevant experience (purposive
sampling). The citizen developers had each built at least two
applications and were familiar with Claude Code. None of the
participants belonged to the development team. The software engineers
had also carried out the IT reviews, so their views are informed but
not independent of them. Following the idea of information power
\parencite{malterud2016informationpower}, a small sample is sufficient
when the aim is narrow and the participants are highly experienced.
The study does not claim that the sample is representative.

All interviews followed the same semi-structured procedure. Each
interview began with a short slide presentation of the final framework
version, followed by an example walkthrough showing how the framework
would be used in daily work. Participants were then asked about its
clarity, usefulness, feasibility and gaps, and about the resources and
organisational conditions it would need. Interviews with software
engineers took about one hour and also covered the code quality of the
three applications, in order to compare it across the applications.
Interviews with citizen developers took about 30 to 45 minutes and
focused on whether the framework addresses problems they had
experienced. The interview guide is in Appendix~[X].

Several measures were taken to reduce bias. Because the researcher
designed the framework and also conducted the interviews, participants
might have avoided criticism. Every interview therefore asked directly
about weaknesses and missing elements. Some participants had given
ideas or tried parts of the framework earlier; this is treated as
background, not as an additional test, and is kept separate from their
final assessment where possible.

The interviews were recorded and handled in line with data protection
requirements. Participation was voluntary, and participants agreed
verbally to recording and transcription at the start of each interview.
Interviews were transcribed with the transcription feature of Microsoft
Teams. Generative AI helped summarise the transcripts. The researcher
checked every summary against the original statements to avoid
inaccurate summaries or wrong conclusions. Only tools approved by SIXT
were used. Participants are identified only by their codes (E1--E3 and
C1--C5), and the thesis contains no personal HR data.


\subsection{Data Analysis and Evidence Integration}
\label{sec:method-analysis}
 
The analysis follows the three phases and is organised around the
difficulties identified in App~1. For each difficulty, it traces the
initial observation, the reason for the framework response, the
evidence from applying that response and any remaining limitation.
 
\textbf{Phase 1.} The App~1 evidence is organised into a list of
difficulties. Each difficulty is supported by at least one source, such
as user feedback, the IT review, discarded work or a request for help.
 
\textbf{Phase 2.} Each difficulty is compared with the literature to
find practices that address it. The design rationale in
Section~\ref{sec:framework} records, for each practice, the difficulty
it addresses and its sources. Results from trying selected practices
in App~2 are reported where they changed the design.
 
\textbf{Phase 3.} The App~3 evidence is analysed in two ways. First, a
list shows for each practice whether it was applied, partly applied,
not applied or not tested. Second, the App~3 results are compared with
App~1 for each evaluation criterion, with App~2 as an intermediate
point. The final interviews are analysed with thematic analysis
\parencite{braun2006thematic}. Coding starts with the topics clarity,
usefulness, feasibility, gaps and organisational conditions, and new
themes are added when statements do not fit. The researcher coded all
data alone.
 
\textbf{Combining the evidence.} Application evidence and interview
evidence are first analysed separately and then compared. This allows
the findings to distinguish three cases: a practice that was adopted, a
practice that demonstrably helped in a particular case, and a practice
whose value practitioners expected but whose effect remains untested.
Different sources can complement each other. For example, a user
walkthrough can show that a user completed an HR task, a test with
different access roles can show that an access rule held under the
tested conditions, and an engineer can explain why this check matters
for release. Reported together, these findings connect business
function, technical verification and expert judgement, while each keeps
its own scope.
 
Where the sources agree, a cautious conclusion is drawn. Where they
disagree, the conflict is retained and reported as a limitation or open
question. Where the same engineer reviewed the code and later discussed
it in an interview, the interview adds interpretation to that review
rather than independent confirmation. Three rules apply: interview
opinions are not counted as application tests, expected benefits are
not treated as observed results, and a positive IT review does not
confirm every governance practice. Each conclusion in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} states whether it rests on application
evidence, interview evidence, both, or neither. Suggestions made after
the final framework version was fixed on the start of November are reported as future
work.

\subsection{Conceptual Foundations and Key Definitions}
AI-assisted, agentic, and vibe coding. AI-supported software development today is not one single thing. It runs along a spectrum defined by degrees of AI autonomy, interaction modalities, and human oversight \parencite{farrag2026,mandl2026,sapkota2025}. 
Grounded in the AI-Augmented Methodology Taxonomy (AAMT) proposed by \textcite{farrag2026}, this spectrum can be categorized into three distinct tiers:


\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1: Passive Suggestion (AI-Assisted Coding):} At this baseline, Generative AI operates as an inline autocompletion engine within the developer's Integrated Development Environment (IDE) \parencite{farrag2026, diaz2026}. The AI assists with localized tasks such as filling short code blocks, suggesting syntax, or drafting basic unit tests. The human developer remains the primary code author, maintaining direct cognitive engagement and line-by-line inspection \parencite{diaz2026}.
    
    \item \textbf{Tier 2: Active Generation (Vibe Coding):} Coined initially by Karpathy and formalized by \textcite{meske2025}, \textit{vibe coding} shifts software creation from deterministic syntax authoring to probabilistic \textit{intent mediation}. Through multi-turn natural language dialogue, developers articulate high-level goals ("the vibe"), while the AI generates entire components or scripts \parencite{meske2025, farrag2026}. In this mode, the primary human role shifts from direct implementation to conversational orchestration and output review \parencite{diaz2026, sapkota2025}.
    
    \item \textbf{Tier 3: Autonomous Agency (Agentic Coding):} Positioned at the autonomous end of the spectrum, Agentic Software Engineering (ASE) delegates goal-level technical objectives (e.g., refactoring a legacy service or implementing an end-to-end API) to autonomous multi-agent systems \parencite{sapkota2025, diaz2026}. Operating within sandboxed execution environments, agentic systems independently formulate execution plans, modify repository files, invoke terminal commands, run test suites, and repair failing code with minimal step-by-step human intervention \parencite{sapkota2025, mandl2026}.
\end{enumerate}


The labels used for AI-supported software development are not yet stable.
Beyond \enquote{vibe coding} and \enquote{agentic coding}, the literature uses
several partly overlapping terms for systems that plan and carry out software
tasks with limited human intervention. These include \enquote{Agentic Software
Engineering} or \enquote{SE~3.0} \parencite{hassan2025}, \enquote{AI teammates}
\parencite{hassan2024, li2025teammates}, \enquote{autonomous coding agents} \parencite{li2025teammates},
and \enquote{AI software engineer} or \enquote{agentic software engineer}
\parencite{hoda2025}. These terms differ in emphasis rather than in the underlying
phenomenon. Some describe a tool (\emph{coding agent}), some a role
(\emph{AI teammate}, \emph{AI software engineer}), and some a discipline or era
(\emph{Agentic Software Engineering}, \emph{SE~3.0}). \textcite{hassan2025}, for
instance, distinguish one-to-one \enquote{agentic coding} from team-level
\enquote{agentic software engineering}. \textcite{hoda2025} observe that
terminology drift is already visible even though the field is nascent. Even the
term \enquote{vibe coding} is defined inconsistently. \textcite{sapkota2025} frame it
as a human-in-the-loop counterpart to agentic coding, whereas \textcite{ge2025}
define it as the practice of managing coding agents. Because this vocabulary is
still evolving and partly shaped by vendor marketing, this thesis does not adopt
any single label as authoritative. Instead, it uses the terms defined in
Section~X as working definitions and characterises each use case by its degree of
AI autonomy and human engagement with the code, not by its label.
 

On one end sits AI-assisted coding, where AI works as a small helper inside the developer's editor (IDE). It suggests the next line of code, fills in short blocks, writes basic tests, or drafts documentation. The developer still writes and reads the code. On the other end sits agentic coding. Here, the developer gives the AI a high-level goal---for example, ``migrate this repository from Python 2 to Python 3''---and the AI plans the steps, opens files, runs tests in a safe sandbox, reads the error logs, and fixes itself with little human help \parencite{sapkota2025,galster2026}. Between and around these two sits vibe coding (VC), a term coined by Andrej Karpathy in February 2025 \parencite{karpathy2025vibecoding}. \textcite{sarkar2025} describe vibe coding as ``material disengagement'': the programmer no longer works directly on the code itself but steers the AI through natural-language chat, screenshots, and quick ``vibe checks'' on whether the running app feels right.

From chat window to terminal: YOLO, Claude Code, Codex, and MCP. For a long time, using an LLM for coding meant copy-and-paste: the developer copied code into a chat window, got a suggestion, and pasted it back. Newer tools remove this manual loop. Claude Code and OpenAI Codex CLI run directly in the terminal. They can read the whole repository, edit files, run shell commands, and execute tests on their own \parencite{galster2026}. This is often used in "YOLO mode" — the developer accepts all changes without reading each diff, trusting the agent to move forward. On top of this, the Model Context Protocol (MCP) lets the agent connect to external tools and data sources (databases, ticket systems, documentation, other MCP servers) through a standard interface \parencite{galster2026}. In parallel, sub-agents can be spawned for specific jobs (e.g., a reviewer agent, a test-writer agent) and only their final output flows back to the main agent \parencite{galster2026}. This is a real historical shift: from "suggest a snippet in a chat window" to "work autonomously inside my codebase with access to my tools".

Deterministic tools vs. probabilistic foundation models. To understand why this shift matters, it helps to look at what came before. In classical software engineering — and in low-code / no-code (LCNC) platforms — the tools are deterministic. This means the same input always gives the same output, following fixed, hard-coded rules. LCNC platforms use Model-Driven Development (MDD): a citizen developer drags visual blocks onto a canvas to draw the business logic, and the platform translates this visual model into runnable code or database configurations through strict compiler-like rules \parencite{francois2025,gadde2025}. There is no guesswork. The compiler acts as a strict guardrail: it does not let the user produce an invalid SQL statement or an unclosed bracket.

Generative AI works differently. Large Language Models (LLMs) are probabilistic. They do not "know" syntax the way a compiler does. Instead, they use autoregressive next-token prediction: for every next word (token) in the output, they compute the statistical probability of what comes next, based on patterns learned from very large training corpora of code and text \parencite{mandl2026}. Two consequences follow. First, the same natural-language prompt can produce different code on different runs — the model is non-deterministic by design. Second, because the output is what is statistically plausible and not what is mathematically verified, LLMs often produce code that looks clean and professional but contains hallucinated libraries, security holes, or architectural inconsistencies \parencite{farrag2026}.

Why this matters for the SIXT thesis: the Productivity–Reliability Paradox. This deterministic vs. probabilistic split is the root cause of the Productivity–Reliability Paradox (PRP) described by \textcite{farrag2026}. Low-code has built-in guardrails and is therefore safe but slow and narrow. Vibe coding is fast and open but has no built-in guardrails; if the developer blindly accepts every suggestion, the codebase quickly fills up with hidden or "shadow" technical debt \parencite{farrag2026,kobiella2026}. The idea of this thesis is to bridge the two: to take a very fast but volatile probabilistic engine (vibe coding) and wrap it inside a strict deterministic harness — CI/CD pipelines, automated security tests, and expert stage-gates — so that all AI-generated code must pass through fixed, machine-checked rules before it reaches production. In the terms of \textcite{galster2026}, this is harness engineering: configuring the environment around the model (context files, skills, sub-agents, MCP tools, hooks) so the model can move fast without breaking the rules of the enterprise. The goal is simple: the speed of vibe coding, with the safety of classic software engineering.

\subsection{Software development before and after GenAI REVIEWED}

Developing a governance and development framework for citizen developers requires an understanding of how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) changes the software development life cycle (SDLC). This includes both the activities needed to turn a business need into operational software and the distribution of responsibilities across product, design, engineering and operations.

Before the widespread adoption of GenAI coding tools, a representative iterative development cycle began with identifying business and user needs and translating them into prioritised requirements. Product managers and product owners coordinated priorities, designers developed interaction concepts, and software engineers implemented the solution. Implementation involved repeated testing and debugging, followed by code review, integration and deployment. Once the software entered production, operational monitoring, incidents and user feedback informed corrections and new development tasks. These activities formed a continuous feedback cycle rather than a strictly linear sequence. The precise division of responsibilities varied between organisations. This description provides a simplified baseline for the lifecycle activities discussed by \textcite{mandl2026} and the role-specific handoffs examined by \textcite{li2026product}. Figure~\ref{fig:software-factory-before-2022} summarises this baseline software factory.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/software-factory-before-2022.png}
    \caption{A software factory circa 2022: human roles plan work, engineers build and test, and changes reach production via pull requests, CI/CD checks, and human review. Feedback returns through monitoring, incidents, and user complaints or feature requests \parencite{bcgplatinion2026agentic}.}
    \label{fig:software-factory-before-2022}
\end{figure}

Code implementation required substantial time and technical expertise, but it should not be described as the sole universal bottleneck of traditional software development. Delivery also depended on requirements clarification, review, integration, testing and release. Accelerating implementation alone therefore cannot guarantee an equivalent improvement in delivered software. \textcite{demirer2026} explain this through the weak-link hypothesis: gains in one activity are constrained by complementary activities elsewhere in the production process.

With GenAI, users can express requirements in natural language and ask coding assistants or agents to generate, modify and test implementations. \textcite{meske2025} describe this as a shift in how human intent is translated into software: people increasingly communicate their goals through dialogue, while AI systems infer and produce the corresponding implementation. In product teams, \textcite{li2026product} identify an iterative workflow comprising ideation, generation, debugging, and testing and review. These activities can overlap more closely because a functional prototype can be generated and evaluated directly, reducing some of the handoffs between initial ideas, design and implementation. Figure~\ref{fig:software-factory-with-agents} illustrates the agentic software factory in which agents perform much of the build and testing work.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/software-factory-with-agents.png}
    \caption{The agentic software factory: agents perform the build and much of the testing (orchestration, harness, sandbox, model), compressing implementation to minutes or hours, while humans retain review and testing before rollout to production \parencite{bcgplatinion2026agentic}.}
    \label{fig:software-factory-with-agents}
\end{figure}


The wider development lifecycle nevertheless remains necessary. Generated changes must still be assessed against requirements, checked for technical quality, integrated with existing systems and prepared for deployment. After deployment, monitoring and user feedback continue to identify defects and new requirements. GenAI can support these activities, including test generation, review, diagnosis and maintenance, but its outputs still require appropriate verification and accountable decisions \parencite{mandl2026}. Faster prototyping consequently does not establish production readiness: \textcite{kobiella2026} find that non-technical users can create useful prototypes while continuing to encounter substantial barriers in deployment and long-term maintenance.

As code generation accelerates, review, verification and integration can become more prominent bottlenecks. In their working paper, \textcite{demirer2026} report cumulative increases of approximately 240\% in commits across successive generations of AI coding tools, compared with approximately 30\% in releases. These are different output measures, indicating that increased coding activity translates only partly into shipped software. More directly relevant to differences in experience, \textcite{asdaque2026} find that contributions from less-experienced AI-assisted developers attract more review comments and take longer to resolve than those from more-experienced contributors. Their observational findings suggest that easier code generation can shift additional work onto reviewers; they do not establish that the same effects necessarily occur among non-technical employees in an enterprise setting.

A related challenge concerns compounding error and compounding correctness, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:compounding-error-correctness}. These terms are used here as a conceptual synthesis rather than as established quantitative laws. Compounding error describes a pattern in which later changes build on unresolved defects or incorrect assumptions, increasing the difficulty of subsequent diagnosis and correction. This interpretation is consistent with the risks identified by \textcite{meske2025}, including unpredictable rewrites, unintended side effects and accumulating technical debt. Compounding correctness describes the intended alternative: preserving verified behaviour while introducing controlled changes. For example, \textcite{wang2026vibecontract} propose explicit task-level contracts that connect requirements, generated code and continuous verification. Such mechanisms can support cumulative quality improvement, but passing checks cannot guarantee correctness or maintainability.


GenAI also makes some professional boundaries more permeable, particularly during exploration and prototyping. \textcite{li2026product} report that designers, engineers and product managers can explore and develop prototypes with fewer sequential handoffs. Product owners and business specialists can therefore participate more directly in implementation, while engineers can use AI assistance to explore interfaces and work across technical tasks. However, this expansion of participation does not make professional expertise interchangeable. \textcite{kobiella2026} show that non-technical users' ability to produce and assess visible prototype behaviour does not extend equally to evaluating underlying code quality, security or maintainability.
For this thesis, these findings motivate a framework that combines broader participation with explicit responsibilities and verification requirements. Citizen developers can contribute domain knowledge, explore solutions and assess whether prototypes meet business needs. Progress towards production requires additional technical, organisational and operational support, alongside appropriately qualified review.

Beyond supporting individual development tasks, a longer-term ambition is to automate much of the software lifecycle through coordinated AI agents. In this vision of an agentic software factory, agents translate approved requirements into implementations, execute tests, address failures, and support deployment and maintenance within defined constraints. The prospect of connecting these activities into an increasingly autonomous process helps explain the growing interest in agentic software factories \parencite{mandl2026}. However, extensive automation remains an aspiration rather than an established capability across enterprise contexts. Human goal-setting, governance and accountability remain necessary, even where execution becomes largely autonomous \parencite{mandl2026,meske2025}. For this thesis, this vision provides a direction of development; the framework must establish which activities can already be delegated reliably and where expert intervention remains required.

An enabling environment is needed because faster code generation does
not by itself lead to faster delivery of usable software. Gains in one
activity are limited by complementary activities, such as review,
testing and integration \parencite{demirer2026}, and AI tends to amplify
the existing strengths and weaknesses of an organisation
\parencite{dora2025}. Without shared rules, checks and version control,
early errors can build on each other; a stable environment instead
allows verified results to accumulate
(Section~\ref{sec:compounding-error-correctness}).

The five layers are a design decision of this thesis. No single source
proposes them, and the assignment of each element to a layer is also a
design choice. The layering serves three purposes. First, it makes the
framework easier to understand, because each layer answers one
question: who decides, what is provided, how agents are steered and
checked, how people are enabled, and how one application moves forward.
Second, it allows an organisation to implement the framework step by
step, starting with the lower layers. Third, it separates
responsibilities. Each element belongs to exactly one layer, and the
layers are intended not to overlap and, together, to cover the
conditions identified in this thesis. Because each layer requires
different knowledge and skills, from governance and law to platform
operation, agent configuration and teaching, each layer can have an
identifiable owner (Table~\ref{tab:framework-layers}). One person or
unit may nevertheless own several layers.

Higher layers build on lower ones. A missing layer does not necessarily
block the layers above it, but it can undermine them, because decisions
in a lower layer limit what is responsible in a higher one. Isolated
environments (Layer~2) illustrate this. Coding agents can run commands,
edit files and access connected systems with little supervision
\parencite{galster2026}, and they sometimes violate constraints or
change their environment in unintended ways
\parencite{alhasan2026,uddin2026}. In a sandbox with synthetic data,
such errors remain contained. Without one, giving agents this freedom
(Layer~3) would be irresponsible, because an agent acting outside its
task could publish or delete employee data. A practitioner in
\textcite{viljoen2024} raised a similar concern about people with little
programming experience who have unrestricted access to company data.
Whether real data may enter such an environment at all is in turn
decided by the data rules in Layer~1.

The dependency describes a logical order, not a strict sequence. Some
layers can be built in parallel; training (Layer~4), for example, can
start before the harness (Layer~3) is complete. An incomplete
environment also does not mean that citizen development must stop. It
limits what may be built: an organisation can start with low-risk
applications and widen the scope as further layers become available.
The infrastructure check in Stage~1 applies this rule to each
application (Section~\ref{sec:working-practices}).

Section~\ref{sec:enabling-environment} describes Layers~1 to~4, and
Section~\ref{sec:working-practices} describes the working practices.
Which elements already existed at SIXT, which are proposed by this
thesis, and how they were used in the three applications is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\paragraph{Risk-Tier Scheme}
\label{sec:risk-tiers}

The risk-tier scheme defines how much control an application and each of
its changes need. It is set once for all citizen-developed applications.
Applying it to one application, for example deciding at G1 that an
application is R2, is part of the working practices
(Section~\ref{sec:working-practices}). Matching control to risk allows
low-risk applications to pass with little effort and focuses scarce
expert time where it is needed, because human oversight is costly when it
is applied to every case in the same way \parencite{kang2026}.

\subparagraph{What Risk Means}

In this framework, risk is the potential harm that an application built
with GenAI may cause if it fails, works incorrectly or is misused. Such
harm includes exposed or wrong data, a disrupted business process and the
breach of internal rules or legal requirements. Risks that arise from AI
functions inside the application, such as a chatbot giving wrong answers,
are outside this scheme (Section~\ref{sec:scope}). An application with
such functions is additionally referred to the organisation's governance
for AI systems, for example a method such as MeRGE \parencite{fetzer2026}.

\subparagraph{Risk Tiers and Risk Classes}

The scheme works on two levels. Both use the labels R0 to R3, which
follow the four levels of Agentic Agile-V \parencite{koch2026agentic},
but each level has its own criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The \emph{risk tier} of an application describes the impact of
    the application as a whole. It decides how much support IT provides,
    who decides at the gates, which evidence is required and which
    infrastructure and skills must be available.
    \item The \emph{risk class} of a change describes the type of a single
    change, such as a text correction or a new function. It decides how
    closely the change is reviewed at Checkpoint~B.
\end{itemize}

Two levels are needed because the impact of an application and the type
of a change do not always match. A text correction in a high-impact
application needs little review, whereas a change to the access rules of
a moderate-impact application needs close review. A change can therefore
receive a higher or a lower class than the tier of its application. A
higher class makes the review of that change stricter, but it does not
change the tier of the application, unless the change also changes the
impact of the application, for example by adding write access to another
system. In that case, the tier is reassessed. This separation into two
levels is a design decision of this thesis.

At the application level, R0 (\emph{exploratory}) describes a status
rather than an impact. It covers prototypes and demonstrations that use
only synthetic or anonymised data and have no real users. At G1, the IT
reviewer therefore sets a provisional \emph{target tier} from R1 to R3,
which is the tier the application would have in real use. Until G2, all
work runs under R0 conditions, whatever the target tier. The three
target tiers are R1 (\emph{low impact}), R2 (\emph{moderate impact}) and
R3 (\emph{high impact}).

\subparagraph{Tier Factors}

The tier of an application is judged by seven factors
(Table~\ref{tab:tier-factors}). Four of them follow \textcite{kang2026},
who classify AI-generated code by data sensitivity, regulatory impact,
proximity to customers and reversibility. For internal applications,
proximity to customers is adapted here as the reach of the users.
Security-sensitive functions, such as login and access rights, are added
because they raise the risk of a change \parencite{koch2026agentic}. The
connection to other systems and the dependence of business processes are
added because research on low-code development recommends that IT
co-develops or leads when an application needs complex integrations or
supports mission-critical or cross-functional processes
\parencite{viljoen2024}. The selection of the factors and their levels
is a design decision of this thesis.

Data sensitivity is judged with the data classification that the
organisation already uses, for example \emph{public}, \emph{internal},
\emph{confidential} and \emph{strictly confidential}. Defining such a
classification is beyond the scope of this thesis. For readability,
Table~\ref{tab:tier-factors} uses three simplified levels: no personal
data, ordinary personal data and sensitive personal data.

\subparagraph{Combining the Factors}

The highest-rated factor determines the tier of the whole application.
The ratings are not averaged. The same rule, called the \enquote{high
water mark}, is used in established security standards: a system
receives the highest impact level found among its types of information
and its security objectives \parencite{nist2004fips199,nist2006fips200}.

A simple example illustrates the rule. A tool for planning team events
is used by one team, nothing depends on it and mistakes are easy to fix.
On these factors, it is R1. To organise shared transport, however, it
imports the home addresses of employees from the HR system. Because of
this one function, it processes personal data and reads from a system of
record, so the whole tool becomes R2. A parcel works the same way: if it
contains one fragile item, the whole parcel is handled as fragile. An
average would hide exactly the factor that matters. If the tool did not
need the addresses, leaving them out would keep it at R1; restricting
the scope in this way is often a better choice than accepting a higher
tier. If a factor cannot be judged with confidence, the higher level is
assumed \parencite{kang2026}.

\subparagraph{Ownership, Assignment and Reassessment}

The scheme is owned by IT management together with information security
and data protection. They define the factors, levels and requirements,
and they revise them when experience shows that a tier is too strict or
too loose.

The tier of an application is assigned by the IT reviewer, not by the
citizen developer. This keeps building and approving separate
\parencite{kang2026}. To keep the effort low, the citizen developer
answers a short questionnaire with one question per factor, and the IT
reviewer sets the tier from these answers. The questionnaire is a design
proposal of this thesis.

A tier is not permanent. The following events trigger a reassessment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a new type of data, especially personal data;
    \item a new connection to another system, or write access to it;
    \item a new user group or a wider rollout;
    \item new roles or broader access rights;
    \item a serious incident or repeated errors in operation;
    \item a change in the business purpose of the application.
\end{itemize}

During development, the tier is reassessed at the next gate. During
operation, the operations owner reports the trigger and the tier is
reassessed at once. A tier can also be lowered, for example when the
scope is restricted. The list of triggers is a design decision of this
thesis.

\subparagraph{Limits of the Scheme}

The factors, their levels and the requirements for each tier are design
proposals of this thesis and have not been validated. The sources they
build on were not developed for citizen development: the levels of
Agentic Agile-V address AI-assisted development by professional teams
\parencite{koch2026agentic}, and the high water mark comes from security
standards for information systems
\parencite{nist2004fips199,nist2006fips200}. Concrete thresholds, such
as what counts as a defined group of users, depend on the organisation.
How the scheme was applied to the three applications at SIXT is reported
in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\paragraph{Verification over Plausibility}

\paragraph{Compounding Error versus Compounding Correctness}
\label{sec:compounding-error-correctness}
Later corrections are especially hard for citizen developers after a production build has begun.
This difficulty can be explained through two possible development patterns: compounding error and compounding correctness. These terms, which are common in the practice of AI-assisted development \parencite{strongdm2026SoftwareFactory}, are used here as a synthesis of sources rather than as established or quantitatively validated laws (see Figure~\ref{fig:compounding-error-correctness}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/fig-compounding-error-correctness.jpg}
    \caption{Conceptual illustration of compounding correctness and compounding error in AI-assisted development (own illustration).}
    \label{fig:compounding-error-correctness}
\end{figure}

Without structural guardrails, unstructured vibe coding can create a negative feedback loop. Initial defects, unstated assumptions and hallucinated logic may be carried forward and build on each other across multi-turn interactions, making subsequent changes harder. \textcite{sarkar2025} describe “context momentum”: earlier prompts and generated outputs influence later development and can make it difficult to move away from initial implementation choices. In their example, the model interpreted a request for historical exchange rates as a single date. When the developer later explicitly requested a date range, the newly generated code still fetched only one date. If such choices are made directly in the production build, they may become fixed before the requirements are fully understood. A related concern is epistemological debt, which \textcite{ginac2026} defines as the gap between a system’s behaviour and its maintainers’ understanding of it. This conceptual framework suggests that accepting generated changes without maintaining that understanding can weaken the ability to diagnose and correct failures. It does not establish that errors inevitably multiply with every interaction. Prototyping can partly address both concerns: implementation choices are explored before the production build, and citizen developers can understand the intended behaviour before production code is generated.

 When vibe coding is bounded by a technical harness, explicit specifications and verification against ground truth, later iterations can build on validated results. Correctness and quality may then accumulate over time. A technical harness is a configured environment of tools, constraints and automated checks. A validated prototype allows production development to start from a reviewed basis. When the specification derived from the prototype is reviewed, changes are checked against explicit expected behaviour, and validated corrections are retained in tests and rules, later iterations can build on earlier improvements. In an industry experience report, \textcite{lopopolo2026HarnessEngineering} describes using enforceable architectural constraints, repository documentation and automated feedback to guide coding agents. Similarly, \textcite{wang2026vibecontract} proposes developer-validated contracts that connect intended behaviour with code generation, testing and verification. VibeContract is a vision paper supported by an illustrative example, rather than broad empirical validation. In the proposed framework, the validated prototype is the source of this intended behaviour. Together, these sources motivate the possibility of cumulative quality improvements. They do not guarantee that every iteration increases correctness.

\paragraph{Separation of Building and Approving}

\subsubsection{Stage-Gate Foundation}

\paragraph{Rationale for a Stage-Gate Process}

AI-assisted development changes where the cost of software lies. Generating code has become fast and cheap (see paragraph software dev before and after GenAI TBD). Specifying, verifying, integrating and taking responsibility for that code has not, and these activities now make up a larger share of the total cost of a change \parencite{wangliu2026}. When more code is produced than can be checked, teams build up \emph{verification debt}: weak tests, hidden regressions and a growing review burden. The time needed for review and clean-up can cancel out the speed gained through AI \parencite{koch2026agentic}.

For citizen developers, these risks are higher. They can judge whether software looks and behaves as intended, but not whether the code is secure or maintainable. They also find the step from a prototype to a deployed product especially difficult \parencite{kobiella2026}. Applications built outside IT can turn into shadow IT and technical debt that the organisation later has to maintain \parencite{viljoen2024}.

These conditions call for an approach that limits investment until uncertainty has been reduced. A stage-gate process does this. Work is divided into stages, and a gate between two stages decides whether the work may continue. Each gate unlocks more resources: time, budget, data access, technical support and users. Early stages are cheap and tightly restricted. Later stages receive more support but must show more evidence. An idea therefore does not receive production-level support just because it is attractive, and a prototype is not released just because it works.

Gates also make it possible to match oversight to risk. Human oversight adds value, but it is costly when applied to every task in the same way \parencite{kang2026}. Reviewing everything in depth would overload scarce experts, while reviewing too little would let risky applications reach users. In addition, each gate has a named decision-maker. This prevents an application from being released before anyone has accepted responsibility for its remaining risks \parencite{wangliu2026}.

The process must, however, remain lightweight. Excessive control can demotivate citizen developers, whose participation is often voluntary \parencite{viljoen2024}. The framework therefore keeps the number of stages small, uses the same decision logic at every gate and lets low-risk applications pass with little effort.

The logic of deciding early before investing more is also found in MeRGE, the Method for Responsible GenAI-Based Information Systems Engineering. Its first phase checks whether a use case is realistic, in order to avoid unnecessary, time-consuming and resource-intensive work later \parencite{fetzer2026}. MeRGE was, however, not designed for vibe coding or citizen development. It guides organisations in engineering information systems that contain GenAI, such as an HR chatbot based on a large language model. \textcite{fetzer2026} developed it in a twelve-month action design research project at a German premium car manufacturer. This thesis addresses a different object: not software that contains GenAI, but software that non-technical employees build using GenAI. MeRGE therefore does not cover the controls that matter most here, such as how AI-generated code is verified against requirements or how a citizen-built prototype moves into production development. It is also not a formal gate framework with pass and fail criteria, and it excludes the maintenance of systems after deployment \parencite{fetzer2026}.

MeRGE is nevertheless used in this thesis because its organisational logic does not depend on this difference. Deciding early whether a use case is worth pursuing, linking business value with data sensitivity and risk, involving experts at defined points and introducing software through a controlled pilot apply to any internally developed software. MeRGE is also close to the setting of this thesis: it was developed with action design research in a large German company and includes an HR use case. This thesis therefore adopts MeRGE selectively. Its Precheck and Conceptualization phases inform the early screening at G1 and G2, and its Quality and Deployment phases inform the readiness checks, approvals and pilot at G4 and G5. Its GenAI-specific measures, such as protection against harmful outputs, are not adopted. The transition from prototype to production, the controls for AI-assisted coding and the operation stage are added by this thesis.

\paragraph{Overall Structure}
\label{sec:stage-gate-structure}

The framework moves an application through six stages, each followed by a gate (Table~\ref{tab:stage-gate-overview}).

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \small
    \caption{Stages and gates of the framework (own representation).}
    \label{tab:stage-gate-overview}
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.0cm} p{6.4cm} p{4.2cm}}
    \hline
    \textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Main activity} & \textbf{Gate question} \\

The framework distinguishes three units of work. The \emph{application}
is the whole tool and passes through one stage-gate lifecycle, from idea
to retirement. A \emph{change} is one meaningful piece of work on the
application, such as a new feature, a larger extension or a bug fix. The
first build in stage~3 consists of a series of changes, and updates
during the pilot and operation are further changes. Each change passes
its own Agile-V cycle, from Checkpoint~A to Checkpoint~B. A \emph{task}
is a small step within a change, which the Build Agent carries out
through the SCOPE-V cycle. In a leave-request tool, for example, the
tool is the application, submitting a leave request is a change, and
ensuring that the leave balance can never become negative is a task.
This division into three units is a design decision of this thesis.

Stages~2 and~3 are described in detail in Sections~TBD. The other stages build on MeRGE, which moves from an early check through conceptualisation, development and quality assurance to deployment \parencite{fetzer2026}. The framework departs from MeRGE in two ways. First, it adds a separate test stage for the whole application, because accepting individual changes does not show that the application works as a whole. Second, it adds an operation stage, because MeRGE excludes maintenance \parencite{fetzer2026}, while AI-built applications in particular risk losing the shared understanding of why they exist and how they work \parencite{storey2026}.

The stage-gate process serves as the macro framework of this thesis. It steers the application as a whole and decides at each gate whether it may advance. Within individual stages, it adopts smaller frameworks that guide the detailed work. In stage~2, a lightweight V-shaped cycle adapted from Agile~V guides how the prototype is specified, built and evaluated \parencite{koch2026agile}. In stage~3 and for later updates, a fuller Agile-V cycle guides each change, and the SCOPE-V cycle guides each task within it \parencite{koch2026agile,koch2026agentic}. These smaller frameworks decide only about their own level of work. Accepting a change therefore never authorises a release; only the gates of the macro framework do.

\paragraph{Gate Decisions}

Every gate uses the same five outcomes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Go:} the work moves to the next stage and receives the resources it needs.
    \item \textbf{Revise:} the work stays in the current stage until open points are resolved.
    \item \textbf{Restrict:} the work continues within narrower limits, for example fewer users or no personal data.
    \item \textbf{Hand over:} the work moves to IT or professional developers, because it exceeds what citizen development can safely deliver.
    \item \textbf{Stop:} the work ends, and what was learned is recorded.
\end{itemize}

Decisions are based on evidence, not on impressions. This follows the acceptance rule of Agentic Agile-V: AI-generated output is accepted not because it looks plausible but because it provides the evidence required for its risk class \parencite{koch2026agentic}. The gates apply the same rule to the application as a whole, with the required evidence set by its risk tier. Each stage therefore has \emph{entry criteria}, which the previous gate must have confirmed, and \emph{exit criteria}, which the next gate checks. Stop criteria, such as an exceeded budget, are defined at the start. Although the stages are shown in sequence, work may return to an earlier stage when new findings require it, as MeRGE also recommends \parencite{fetzer2026}.

\paragraph{Risk-Based Decisions}

How strict a decision is depends on risk, as defined in the risk-tier
scheme (Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiers}). The application receives a risk
tier: the IT reviewer sets a provisional target tier at G1, confirms it
at G2 and reviews it at later gates. Until G2, all work runs under R0
conditions, whatever the target tier. The tier decides who must approve
at the gates and how much evidence is required. Each change receives a
risk class at Checkpoint~A, which decides how closely the change is
reviewed at Checkpoint~B. A change may receive a higher class than the
tier of its application, for example an R3 change to the role logic of
an R2 application; this deepens the review of that change only. The tier
is reassessed only if a change alters the impact of the application as
a whole.

\paragraph{Roles and Responsibilities}

The framework distinguishes seven roles (Table~\ref{tab:stage-gate-roles}). In the setting of this thesis, the end user, the citizen developer and the business owner are often the same person: an HR employee who builds a tool to solve a problem in their own daily work. These roles can, however, be separated. The end users may be colleagues in the same department or in another team. The citizen developer may not use the application much themselves but be responsible for the problem it solves, or may support a product manager or process owner in building it. The roles are therefore described separately, even though one person can hold several of them. When they overlap, the separation of building and approving still applies to the risk tier and the technical approval, which always remain with IT and the relevant specialists.

Four principles guide this distribution. First, business ownership stays with the business: the citizen developer defines what the application should do, and the business owner remains accountable for it. Second, technical and legal judgement stays with specialists. This mirrors the division in spec-driven development, where technical leads own the system specification and developers own the specification of their change \parencite{diaz2026}. Third, building and approving are separated: whoever produces a result does not approve its technical quality or its release \parencite{kang2026}. The citizen developer may therefore accept a change from a business perspective, but does not set its risk class or approve it technically. AI agents never hold approval authority, also because their claims that a task is complete are not always reliable \parencite{alhasan2026}. Fourth, every gate has a named decision-maker, and the set of decision-makers grows with the risk tier.

The stages, gates, roles and tiers described here combine ideas from the cited sources, but their specific arrangement is a design choice of this thesis. Whether it works in practice, and whether its effort is acceptable for citizen developers and experts, is evaluated through ADR (Section~TBD).

\subsubsection{The Stages}

\paragraph{Stage 1: Screen and Scope}

The first stage decides, with little effort, whether an idea should be pursued through citizen development and under which limits. It produces a short description of the idea, a provisional target tier and the boundaries for the prototype experiment in stage~2. The citizen developer and the business owner describe the idea. The IT reviewer assesses its technical fit and sets the provisional target tier. Specialists are involved only when the screening raises legal or data protection questions.

\subparagraph{Problem and Business Case}

The citizen developer first describes the problem, the intended users and what success would look like, before discussing any solution. MeRGE uses a use-case canvas for this purpose, covering the problem, the value proposition and the risks \parencite{fetzer2026}. IACDM likewise argues that the problem must be understood before technical decisions are made \parencite{moreira2026}.

The business owner then compares the expected benefit with the expected effort. AI makes development cheaper, so problems that used to be too small for professional development may now be worth solving. It does not, however, make development free. The effort for specification, review, testing and later maintenance remains and is easily underestimated \parencite{wangliu2026}. In a controlled trial, experienced developers even worked more slowly with AI while believing they were faster \parencite{moreira2026}. The estimate therefore includes the time of the citizen developer, tool costs, the review effort of IT and specialists, and the operation of the application after release. Expert time, not code generation, is usually the scarcest resource.

\subparagraph{Suitability for Citizen Development}

Not every application should be built by citizen developers. Research on low-code development shows that IT should co-develop or lead when an application requires complex integrations, must meet enterprise standards or demands technical expertise.Mission-critical or cross-functional processes also need more standardisation than isolated tasks \parencite{viljoen2024}. Although this research concerns low-code platforms rather than AI-assisted coding, the same reasons apply. They are even stronger here, because citizen developers cannot inspect the generated code \parencite{kobiella2026}. MeRGE shows a related decision: the planned SAP integration of the HR assistant was postponed because it would have raised the criticality of the processed data \parencite{fetzer2026}. Studies with non-programmers point in the same direction. They performed well on small, fully specified tasks \parencite{esnaola2026}, but features that combined several systems required many iterations \parencite{fortesferreira2025}. Table~\ref{tab:stage1-warning-signs} lists the warning signs used in this stage.

A warning sign does not always end an idea. Often the scope can be reduced so that the risky part is left out or postponed, as in the MeRGE example.

\subparagraph{Reuse and Technical Complexity}

Before anything new is built, the citizen developer and IT check whether a similar application, an existing  tool or a standard product or open source software already solves the problem. MeRGE includes this as a make-or-buy decision that considers the technologies already available in the organisation \parencite{fetzer2026}. IACDM applies the same idea to individual components: if a mature library exists, it should be used; if not, a small proof of concept comes first \parencite{moreira2026}. This matters for AI-assisted development, because AI agents tend to produce plausible new code for problems that already have a tested solution \parencite{moreira2026}.

IT then judges the technical complexity: the required integrations, the data model, the number of user roles and whether the application fits the approved platform and technology stack. IACDM suggests judging complexity from observable signals rather than from estimated code size, with a simple rule: if a project cannot confidently be called simple, it should be treated as complex \parencite{moreira2026}.

\subparagraph{Data, Risk and Legal Requirements}

The data that the application would process are classified first, as in
the first phase of MeRGE \parencite{fetzer2026}. Based on this and the
other factors of the risk-tier scheme, the IT reviewer sets a provisional
target tier (Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiers}). Internal policies and legal
requirements, such as data protection rules or the involvement of the
works council, are identified early, because they can make a use case
impossible to realise \parencite{fetzer2026}.

\subparagraph{Capability of the Citizen Developer}

Citizen developers differ in their technical knowledge, their ability and willingness to learn, and the time they can invest. These differences shape what they can build safely. Users with basic coding knowledge were able to make small changes to generated code themselves, while non-technical users struggled \parencite{kobiella2026}. Non-programmers also often lacked the vocabulary to express their intent \parencite{feldman2024}. Low-code research therefore recommends tailoring governance to the people who build \parencite{viljoen2024}, which also apply here in this case. The following examples show how specific skills change what a citizen developer can do with a coding agent such as Claude Code:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Terminal and coding agent.} Claude Code runs in the terminal and asks for permission before it runs a command or edits a file \parencite{galster2026}. A citizen developer who does not understand these commands cannot judge whether a request is harmless, such as running the tests, or risky, such as deleting files or installing an unknown package. They are then likely to approve every request, which amounts to the \enquote{YOLO mode} described in Section~TBD. Someone with basic terminal knowledge can recognise risky commands and reject them.
    \item \textbf{Git.} Suppose a new feature breaks a part of the application that worked yesterday. Without Git, the citizen developer must ask the agent to repair its own mistake, which may cause further changes, or start again. With basic Git knowledge, they can return to the last working version in one step, compare what has changed and keep the specification and plan versioned together with the code.
    \item \textbf{IDE.} Agents sometimes change files outside their task or claim that a task is complete when it is not \parencite{alhasan2026,uddin2026}. Without an IDE, the citizen developer must rely on the agent's own summary. In an IDE, they can see which files were changed, notice an unexpected change to an unrelated part of the application, run the tests and read the error messages themselves.
    \item \textbf{Basic coding concepts.} A citizen developer who understands functions, data structures or interfaces can follow the technical part of the plan, notice obvious mistakes, such as a password written directly into the code, and give the agent more precise instructions.
    \item \textbf{Text only.} A citizen developer without any of these skills can still describe requirements and judge a prototype in stage~2. For production development, however, they would depend on IT for every technical step.
    \item \textbf{Agents and context windows.} A coding agent only knows what is in its current context: the conversation so far, the files it has read and its rule files. Model performance tends to decline as this context grows longer and mixes unrelated information \parencite{hong2025context}, and earlier prompts can keep steering later output even after the requirements have changed \parencite{sarkar2025}. A citizen developer who does not know this may keep working in one very long chat and wonder why the agent forgets earlier decisions or repeats old mistakes. Someone who understands context windows knows when to start a fresh session, why the framework hands work over through the specification and plan.md rather than through the chat history, and why each agent role works in its own session (Section~TBD).
\end{itemize}

At a minimum, every citizen developer must understand the basic concepts of software development (e.g., how Git works, how to code with agents, and what a terminal is), as well as the process behind this framework (e.g., why specifications are written, what the checkpoints and gates decide, and how evidence is used). Without this understanding, the work is likely to drift back into unstructured vibe coding. This thesis therefore proposes a short capability assessment, for example a self-assessment followed by a brief conversation with an IT mentor. Based on the result, the risk tier and the complexity of the application, the citizen developer may be required to complete certain IT courses and pass their tests before the relevant stage begins, for example a course on the framework process before stage~2 and a course on Git and the coding agent before stage~3 (Section~TBD). The assessment does not decide who may take part. It decides which training and support are needed, such as courses, mentoring or co-development with IT, and up to which risk tier the citizen developer can work without close support. The tests confirm the knowledge taught in the courses and are not used to evaluate employees' performance. The assessment and the course requirements are design proposals of this thesis.


The capabilities of citizen developers should not be seen as fixed. With GenAI itself, online learning resources and internal training, almost all of the concepts above can be learned by citizen developers who are willing to learn and have the time to do so. A citizen developer can, for example, ask an AI assistant to explain an unfamiliar command before approving it, or to walk them through a Git conflict step by step. Learning can also happen just in time, when a concept is first needed, with support from an IT mentor or an internal assistant. This helps beginners overcome blockers quickly and allows more experienced citizen developers to deepen concepts they understand only partly. The capability assessment therefore describes a starting point rather than a permanent limit, and the support level can be adjusted as the citizen developer gains experience. However, whether this learning effort is economically feasible remains an open question. If a citizen developer needs three months to learn enough to build a simple application, the effort may outweigh the benefit, and handing the work over to IT may be the better choice. For this reason, the expected learning effort is included in the business case at G1. This thesis does not examine in detail how much learning effort is acceptable.

\subparagraph{Infrastructure}

Citizen development is only safe when the technical and governance infrastructure described in Section~XX is in place. Citizen developers need controlled sandboxes and separate environments. Where a platform lacks such safeguards, organisations must add access controls and review gates \parencite{viljoen2024}. A practitioner in the same study warned that people with little programming experience and unrestricted access to company data could unintentionally expose sensitive data \parencite{viljoen2024}. Stage~1 therefore checks whether the infrastructure required for the risk tier is available (Table~\ref{tab:tier-consequences}). If it is not, the idea is restricted to a lower tier, for example to mock-ups with synthetic data, handed over or stopped.


\subparagraph{Experiment Boundaries and Gate G1}

If the idea passes the screening, the business owner and IT agree on the boundaries of the prototype experiment: a time limit, a budget, the approved tools, the data that may be used (normally synthetic or anonymised) and the stop criteria. In line with the R0 conditions of the risk-tier scheme
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiers}), no production credentials are used
\parencite{koch2026agentic}. They also agree whether IT will enable, oversee or co-develop, since this affects later staffing and support \parencite{viljoen2024}.

At G1, the decision-makers check whether:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the problem, users and success criteria are described;
    \item no warning sign remains unresolved;
    \item no existing solution fits better;
    \item the complexity matches the capability and support of the citizen developer;
    \item the expected benefit justifies the expected effort;
    \item the data is classified and a provisional target tier is set;
    \item the infrastructure required for this tier is available;
    \item the experiment boundaries and stop criteria are agreed.
\end{itemize}

If all criteria are met, the idea moves to stage~2 as an R0 experiment. Otherwise, the gate decides to revise, restrict, hand over or stop. The depth of the screening depends on the risk: for a small idea with target tier R1, a one-page description and a short conversation with IT are sufficient.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Prototype}

The prototype may consist of screens, a clickable mock-up or a limited interactive demonstration created with an AI-assisted design or prototyping tool. Its purpose is to clarify the business need, explore the proposed workflow and gather early user feedback before committing to production engineering.  

This step of prototyping is especially critical for non-technical citizen developers, who face distinct communicative and evaluative barriers when building software with AI. In a controlled study with 67 non-programmers, participants struggled to express their intent in natural language and lacked the technical vocabulary that code-generating models respond to \parencite{feldman2024}. In a case study, a person without coding experience could not produce a working driving simulator with a single prompt. Features that combined several systems required many iterations, and vague prompts led to unstable results \parencite{fortesferreira2025}. Even experienced programmers found it difficult to describe visual ideas, such as diagrams, in words \parencite{sarkar2025}. Conversely, non-programmers assisted by a conversational AI performed comparatively well when tasks were small and fully specified with business rules and test cases \parencite{esnaola2026}. 


A prototype addresses these barriers. Instead of describing elements such as navigation, filters or button states in words alone, the citizen developer can show, inspect and correct them step by step. \textcite{kobiella2026} found that AI prototyping tools enabled non-technical users to create high-fidelity prototypes for exploration and stakeholder communication. The same study shows why this form of review suits citizen developers. Non-technical participants could judge whether the output looked as intended, but not whether the underlying code was good, performant or secure \parencite{kobiella2026}. Prototyping therefore relies on a form of validation that citizen developers can actually perform.

Furthermore, a clearly defined prototype is likely to reduce changes during production development. Participants in \textcite{kobiella2026} contrasted the relative ease of building and adding features with the difficulty of modifying or removing them. People with basic coding knowledge could make minor adjustments directly in the code, but non-technical users struggled. One participant noted that without coding experience, even small layout changes would require “even more AI calls” \parencite{kobiella2026}. They also gained little from reading generated code or error messages and when their attempts to fix or improve the code using LLMs failed, many ran out of strategies for what to try next \parencite{feldman2024}. Non-technical users also reported that moving from a prototype to a deployed, working product was very challenging \parencite{kobiella2026}. Clarifying what should be built before production development therefore matters more for citizen developers, because later corrections are harder for them to make.
This difficulty is explained through the patterns of compounding error and compounding correctness in Section~\ref{sec:compounding-error-correctness}.

For the proposed framework, these ideas support placing a prototyping step between the idea and production development especially for citizen developers. During prototyping, the specification and user walkthroughs help identify misunderstandings about what should be built and should reduce rework and improve outcomes for citizen developers. This will be evaluated through ADR.

\subparagraph{V-Shaped Exploration Cycle}

The framework uses a lightweight, V-shaped exploration cycle during prototype development and evaluation. It selectively adapts the connection between requirements and evaluation found in Agile V \parencite{koch2026agile}. It is a design adaptation of this thesis, rather than the complete Agile-V process. The SCOPE-V task cycle, separate verification agents and detailed engineering evidence are reserved for the subsequent production-development stage, as they are not needed at this stage and would only create extra work. The Agile-V framework was chosen because AI-assisted development shifts the main challenge from generating code to verifying that the resulting software meets business requirements, functions reliably and satisfies quality standards. Its main advantage is the direct connection between requirements and verification, ensuring that each development decision can be evaluated against predefined expectations rather than relying on plausible AI-generated outputs. Combining the V-Model's structured verification with Agile's iterative flexibility makes it suitable for both development stages: during prototyping, a lightweight V-cycle supports rapid exploration, feedback and validation of business needs; during production development, more rigorous task-level V-cycles introduce independent testing, traceability and documented evidence. This allows the same underlying principle—verification before acceptance—to guide both stages while adapting the level of control to their different objectives and risks.

Since AI-generated code can appear correct while containing hidden logical errors \textcite{wang2026vibecontract}, we adopt a V-shaped framework in which requirements are defined on the left, the prototype is built at the bottom, and its behaviour is systematically tested against those requirements on the right (see Figure~\ref{fig:prototype-exploration-cycle}).

\subparagraph{Left Side: Specifying the Prototype}

On the left side, the citizen developer explores the business problem with an AI assistant, such as ChatGPT, and relevant stakeholders. The feature set does not need to be known in advance. Exploration may involve discussing requirements, questioning assumptions, identifying edge cases and generating alternative designs. Small, disposable mock-ups may be created to investigate individual screens or interactions. These exploratory outputs help clarify the solution but are not automatically treated as agreed requirements.

Before building the consolidated prototype, the citizen developer converts the findings into a concise, standalone specification. It identifies the intended users, selected features, main user journeys, relevant edge cases, expected outcomes, exclusions and any simulated behaviour. Contradictory or outdated ideas are removed, and unresolved questions are recorded explicitly. The citizen developer reviews the specification for consistency and business relevance. A more experienced colleague or relevant specialist is involved where uncertainty warrants it.

\textcite{koch2026agentic} propose converting exploratory dialogues with different LLMs into a reviewed execution brief before starting the develeopment (in this case, the prototype creation), because long conversations can contain superseded assumptions and implicit constraints. This thesis operationalises that principle through a specification handover into a fresh chat or project context before building the consolidated prototype. Similar practices have been observed in vibe coding: developers deliberately opened a new chat or cleared the AI's context before starting a new phase of work \parencite{sarkar2025}, and non-technical participants used a general-purpose chatbot to turn their ideas into a structured prompt or prototype description for a prototyping tool \parencite{kobiella2026}.
The reviewed specification then becomes the input for the build step at the bottom of the V.

\subparagraph{Bottom: Building the Prototype}

At the bottom of the V, the consolidated prototype is built in a fresh chat or project context using the reviewed specification and deliberately selected reference material. A tool such as Claude Design, Figma or another prototyping environment may support this activity. The exploratory conversation is not carried forward as the authoritative build instruction. Instead, the specification provides a clear baseline for what should be demonstrated. The work remains within the approved experiment boundaries, including permitted tools and data.

\subparagraph{Right Side: Evaluating the Prototype}

On the right side, the citizen developer walks through the specified scenarios with intended users or relevant business stakeholders. Findings are compared with the expected outcomes, and a short record captures useful elements, shortcomings and unanswered questions. If feedback changes the intended behaviour, the specification is updated before the prototype is revised. Minor corrections can continue within the build context. Substantial changes return to specification review and may justify a new build context. These return paths are omitted from the figure for readability.

\paragraph{Stage 3: Build and Verify}

\subparagraph{The Agile V Development Framework}


In the production-development stage, the framework uses a more complete version of Agile V than in the prototype stage. Each change, such as a new feature, a larger extension or a bug fix, now goes through its own small V-cycle. This cycle includes independent testing, traceability (a clear link between each requirement and its tests) and documented evidence. It combines two elements from earlier work. From Agile V, it takes the idea that separate AI agents build the software, write the tests, run the tests and collect the evidence \parencite{koch2026agile}. From Agentic Agile-V, it takes the six-step SCOPE-V task cycle \parencite{koch2026agentic}. These are complemented by spec-driven development, contracts and practitioner approaches to planning, which are introduced below. How these elements are combined, assigned to agents and placed within the stage-gate lifecycle is a design choice of this thesis. It is important to note that the framework builds upon the technical and governance setup provided by the company explained in Section TBD!!.


The cycle is based on three principles. The first is spec-driven development (SDD). Every meaningful change starts from a written specification that is stored and versioned together with the code. This specification acts as the contract between people and AI agents. Code, tests and documentation are derived from it. If the specification and the code disagree, the specification counts as correct \parencite{diaz2026}. Furthermore, the specification documents are complemented by a shared plan for each change \parencite{lopopolo2026HarnessEngineering}. The plan, kept in a file such as plan.md, is created by a Planning Agent from the specification once it has been approved at Checkpoint A. The third principle is contract- and test-based verification. A change is accepted because independent tests and other evidence show that it matches the specification, not because the result looks convincing \parencite{koch2026agentic}. The V-shape links the principles: the left side creates the specification, the bottom builds the change, and the right side checks that the change matches the specification and all the updates are then saved in the plan.md. Most sources behind these ideas are conceptual or based on practitioner experience, and they have not been tested with non-technical users. Their suitability is therefore evaluated through ADR.

As shown in the prototype stage, non-technical participants can judge whether software looks and behaves as intended. A stricter structure is needed at this stage because what has to be checked changes. They cannot judge whether the code behind it is correct, secure or easy to maintain. In other words, citizen developers can mainly check functional requirements, that is, what the software does. Especially with specification-driven development (SDD), a clear specification allows reviewers to check whether the evidence demonstrates that the requirements have been met, instead of trying to work out the intended behaviour from the code \parencite{diaz2026}. Still most non-functional requirements, such as security, data protection, reliability and maintainability, are hidden in the code and lie beyond their judgement. Checking these qualities must therefore be shifted as far as possible to other means: the technical setup provided by the company and department, such as approved platforms and automated checks; AI agents that test and analyse the code; and, where needed, supporting stakeholders such as IT, information security or data protection.

To support the with non-functional requirements, Agile V gives two different jobs to two different agents. A Test Designer writes tests based only on the requirements and never sees the code, while a separate Build Agent writes the code. This separation is meant to prevent tests that simply confirm what the code happens to do instead of what it should do \parencite{koch2026agile}. Confirmed behaviour is kept in tests that every later change must also pass. In this way, quality can build up over time, which is how the framework aims at compounding correctness (see Figure~\ref{fig:compounding-error-correctness}).


The three units of work defined in Section~\ref{sec:stage-gate-structure} determine where each framework applies. In Agentic Agile-V, Agile V steers the project as a whole, while SCOPE-V steers the individual tasks carried out by agents \parencite{koch2026agentic}. In this thesis, the stage-gate lifecycle takes over the steering of the application, including investment, ownership, pilot and release. Agile V therefore moves one level down and steers each change, while SCOPE-V remains at the level of tasks. A V-cycle ends when a tested change is accepted, not when the application is released (see Figure~\ref{fig:adapted-agile-v-cycle}).

\subparagraph{Left Side: Specifying the Change}


Following SDD, the specification is divided into two levels with
different purposes \parencite{diaz2026}. The system specification
describes the stable environment of the application: architecture rules,
coding standards and other rules that every change must respect. It is
stored in a rule file, such as AGENTS.md, which the agents read at the
start of every session. The feature specification describes only the
change currently being built within the Agile~V cycle, including its
acceptance criteria. \textcite{diaz2026} compare these two levels to the
difference between organisational policy and operational work
instructions. Keeping them apart matters: if an agent only receives the
feature specification, it may produce code that works but ignores the
general rules; if it only receives the system specification, it does not
know what to build \parencite{diaz2026}. In their model, system
specifications are typically owned by technical leads and architects,
whereas feature specifications are owned by the developer responsible for
the change.

This thesis adapts this division to citizen development: IT maintains the
non-functional system specification, while citizen developers write the
feature specification for their functional changes. As a result, citizen
developers only need to describe their business need, while the technical
and data-protection rules defined once by IT are given to the agents in
every session. Some non-functional requirements, however, depend on the
specific feature and its business context and are therefore part of the
feature specification. Rules in the system specification only guide the
agents. Practitioners who wrote their security rules into such a file
report that agents can ignore or override instructions, so deterministic
checks must still stop any violation \parencite{TODO-koul2026vibesec}.
Each rule is therefore paired with an automated check in a pipeline that
IT configures once and that runs whenever a new version of the code is
saved (Section~\ref{sec:security-rules}).

The citizen developer is responsible for the feature specification. Starting from the tested prototype and its specification, they describe the business need, the intended users and the expected behaviour. Requirements are written as acceptance criteria in the form Given–When–Then, so that each criterion can be turned directly into a test \parencite{diaz2026}. Regarding the non-functional requiements specific to the feature,  AI-generated requirements often leave out non-functional aspects. Quality requirements such as response time, security or the handling of personal data are therefore written down as short quality scenarios with a measurable target \parencite{waseem2025}. In addition, task-level contracts make hidden business rules explicit \parencite{wang2026vibecontract}. Preconditions state what must be true before an action, postconditions what must be true after it, and invariants what must always remain true, for example that a remaining leave balance can never be negative. An AI assistant can draft these criteria, scenarios and contracts from the prototype specification. The citizen developer then checks them, because they describe business rules and expectations rather than technical details. Technical thresholds are confirmed with IT where needed. The resulting brief also states the goal, what is out of scope, which parts of the application are affected, which evidence is required and the risk class \parencite{koch2026agentic}.

At Checkpoint A, the scope, constraints and risk class are confirmed. Checkpoint A therefore works as the conversation-to-contract gate proposed by \textcite{koch2026agentic}: agents build from the reviewed specification, not from the earlier chat. So that citizen developers do not set their own risk class, the risk class is set by predefined organisational rules rather than by their judgement alone (Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiers}). Routine changes within agreed limits continue under delegated approval rules. Some changes require technical experts: those that bring new risks, affect security-sensitive functions or personal data, have unclear technical requirements or go beyond agreed limits. If later planning reveals new risks, missing requirements or contradictions, the change returns to Checkpoint A.

As mentioned earlier, the specification documents are complemented by a shared plan for each change in the agile V cycle. The plan, kept in a separate file such as plan.md, is created by a Planning Agent from the specification once it has been approved at Checkpoint A. The agent divides the approved change into small, testable tasks, links each task to its requirements and arranges them as an ordered checklist. For each task, it defines a task contract based on the approved specification, the expected observable outcome and the requirements that must be met before moving to the next task. The Planning Agent does not create the tests; the next agent turns these requirements into tests. It does not add new behaviour beyond the approved specification.
Both humans and agents update the plan. After each task is implemented, they record relevant notes and implementation decisions and update the checklist to show progress. This follows the practice of an OpenAI engineering team that built a product entirely with coding agents. The team treated plans as first-class artefacts and captured larger pieces of work in execution plans with progress and decision logs, stored and versioned together with the code \parencite{lopopolo2026HarnessEngineering}. The accompanying guidance describes such plans as documents that are updated at every stopping point so that the work can be resumed from the plan alone \parencite{friel2025}.

In this framework, agents record progress and implementation decisions in the plan rather than changing the approved specification. Changes to the specification must go through Checkpoint A. The plan also helps new sessions or agent tools continue the work, reducing the need for the citizen developer to explain it again. This corresponds to the persistent shared knowledge described in SDD, which different agents and tools can read and update \parencite{diaz2026}. If a handover to the IT department is needed because the work has become too complicated, the plan provides a record of progress and decisions, while version control preserves the history of project files.

The plan consists of two parts. The first part is written in plain language and contains the purpose of the change, the tasks with their task contracts and expected outcomes, the progress, the decisions taken and their reasons, unexpected findings and open questions. The second part contains technical notes for the Build Agent, such as the files and functions to be changed. Before the build starts, the citizen developer reviews the first part, which allows them to check the intended approach before a longer implementation begins \parencite{friel2025}. 

The plan in this framework differs from these practitioner sources in three ways \parencite{lopopolo2026HarnessEngineering,friel2025}. First, it is kept separate from the feature specification. In the original approach, the plan itself serves as the specification. Here, the specification is the approved contract and may only be changed through Checkpoint A, while the plan changes constantly. Second, the original approach instructs agents to resolve ambiguities on their own. In this framework, ambiguities that affect requirements are recorded as open questions and returned to the citizen developer or, where needed, to a specialist. Third, the plan is created by a dedicated Planning Agent, which organises the work but does not write code. This follows the distinction in SDD between a coordinating role and agents that carry out tasks in their own contexts \parencite{diaz2026}. In the original approach, one agent writes and maintains its own plan. Here, the plan is a shared working document: the Build, Verification and Auditing Agents record their progress, results and findings in it, and the citizen developer adds decisions and answers to open questions. Each agent writes only to the parts of the plan that its role allows. Unlike the coordinating agent in Agile V, which assigns work to the specialised agents \parencite{koch2026agile}, the Planning Agent does not instruct the other agents; the reasons for this are explained below. These adaptations are design choices of this thesis. The documents and agents are explained below, and Table~\ref{tab:agile-v-artefacts} gives an overview.

\subparagraph{Bottom: Building the Change}

During the build, the plan is kept up to date after each task, either by the Planning Agent based on the reports of the Build Agent Agent, or directly by the Build Agents. When the change is complete, the Agents adds a short summary of the outcome and the lessons learned. Decisions recorded in the plan become part of the evidence bundle at Checkpoint B, and recurring lessons are moved into the system specification through the Evolve step. Completed plans are archived with the code, so that later changes can build on them. Both practitioner sources describe the work of experienced engineers and not citizen developers \parencite{friel2025,lopopolo2026HarnessEngineering}. Whether this approach is usable for citizen developers is therefore evaluated through ADR.

The Build Agent and the Test Designer Agent then do their work separately and in parallel \parencite{koch2026agile}. The Build Agent carries out the tasks in the order set in the plan. The Test Designer Agent writes tests from the approved specification and from the task contracts and expected outcomes in the first part of the plan. It has no access to the generated code or to the technical part of the plan, since both contain implementation choices. As in the prototype stage, separate working contexts are used on purpose: agents that check the work do not share the working memory of the Build Agent. \textcite{koch2026agentic} recommends separating building and testing agents when the risk is high. This thesis separates them for every change, because independent tests replace the routine code review that citizen developers cannot carry out. However, the separation cannot make the specification complete. If a requirement is wrong or missing, all agents may build on the same mistake. The quality of the specification at Checkpoint A therefore remains critical.

The Build Agent completes each task through the SCOPE-V cycle \parencite{koch2026agentic}. In every session, it reads the system specification together with the feature specification and its current task in the plan. It carries out all six SCOPE-V steps for each task on its own:


\begin{description}
    \item[Specify] turns the task into a short brief with a goal, scope, acceptance criteria and required evidence.
    \item[Constrain] sets limits, for example no changes to unrelated files and no new external libraries without a reason. Changes to security-sensitive code are flagged and handled according to the risk class of the change.
    \item[Orchestrate] defines how the agent works: first examine the existing code, then plan, build in small steps and run quick checks.
    \item[Prove] collects evidence that fits the risk. This can include unit tests (single parts), integration tests (parts working together), regression tests (existing functions still work), automated code analysis and security scans.
    \item[Evolve] feeds confirmed lessons back into instructions, templates and tests, and removes outdated instructions. If the same correction is needed repeatedly, it is turned into a rule in the system specification after review, so that it applies to all future work \parencite{diaz2026}. Such changes to shared instructions or specifications follow the same change controls as other changes.
    \item[Verify] is not a final step. Checks are repeated before, during and after building.
\end{description}


\label{sec:build-self-checks}
While working through a task, the Build Agent tests its own work. These
self-checks belong to the Prove and Verify steps of SCOPE-V
\parencite{koch2026agentic}. They are small and fast tests for one task, and
the Build Agent is in charge of them: it decides which checks are needed,
writes them, runs them and repairs the code until they pass. The citizen
developer does not manage these checks one by one. The Build Agent only
records in \texttt{plan.md} that a task is complete and that its checks have
passed. Agentic Agile-V recommends running such targeted checks after each
small change, so that testing happens inside the agent's work rather than
after it \parencite{koch2026agentic}. Typical self-checks are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unit tests} check the smallest parts of the code, for
    example a function that calculates the remaining leave balance.
    \item \textbf{Existing tests of the changed area} are run again to see
    whether the task has broken something next to it.
    \item \textbf{Code-form checks} (linting and type checks) automatically
    find obvious mistakes, such as a misspelled name or a value of the wrong
    kind.
    \item \textbf{Quick security scans} look for passwords written into the
    code and for patterns that IT has marked as unsafe in the system
    specification.
\end{itemize}

These self-checks give fast feedback and find mistakes where they arise,
while they are still cheap to fix. Their limit is that they are not
independent. The agent that wrote the code also decides what to test, so the
tests may simply confirm what the code does instead of what it should do
\parencite{koch2026agile}. Coding agents also sometimes report a task as
complete when it is not \parencite{alhasan2026,uddin2026}. The self-checks
therefore do not count as evidence for accepting a change. They stay in the
code base and are run again with later changes, but they never replace the
independent tests. Within a single task, these self-checks are sufficient.
Independence is ensured at the level of the whole change, where separate
agents design and run the tests (Section~\ref{sec:testing-verification}).
Table~\ref{tab:self-checks-vs-independent} summarises the difference.

The other agents therefore work on the change as a whole, not on each task. The Test Designer Agent writes its tests once per change, based on the specification and the task contracts, while the Build Agent is building. When the Build Agent has finished all tasks, the Verification Agent runs these tests and the Auditing Agent then collects the evidence on the right side of the agile V framework. This matches the Agile V case study, where one cycle covered a whole piece of work with several requirements, not a single small task \parencite{koch2026agile}.

This set-up has an advantage and a drawback. Only one agent works on each task, which keeps cost and effort low, and the independent checks happen where the change is accepted. However, mistakes that the Build Agent's own checks miss are only found after several tasks. For changes of risk class R3, the independent tests are therefore run after each task or after each milestone in the plan.

 This way of dividing the work between the agents is a design choice of this thesis. The test agent and the verification process will be explained in more detail in Section~TBD, as it is one of the most critical steps in vibe coding \parencite{bcgplatinion2026agentic}.


The agents do not talk to each other directly. Instead, they coordinate through shared, versioned files: the specification, the plan, the tests and the test reports. Each agent starts in a fresh session, reads only the files its role allows and writes its results back to these files. The order in which the agents work is fixed by the V-cycle (planning, then building and test design, then verification, then auditing). No agent decides this order. The citizen developer starts each step with a prompt that they have reviewed. The Planning Agent can suggest these prompts or check prompts the citizen developer has written. Each prompt mainly states which role the agent takes, which files it should read and what result it should produce. Because the prompts are concise, the citizen developer can understand them fully and review them quickly. They then check the outcome of each step before the next step begins.


The citizen developer therefore acts as the accountable owner of the change rather than as a programmer. They define the intent, approve the specification at Checkpoint A, answer open questions, adjust the plan where needed and accept the evidence at Checkpoint B. What they verify themselves is limited to what they can judge: functional behaviour and business rules. Non-functional qualities remain with automated checks, the testing agents and, where needed, IT specialists (see Section~TBD).

This human-in-the-loop design is a deliberate choice. Vision papers propose fully automated development lifecycles in which an orchestrating meta-agent steers all phases, including verification, with little or no human involvement \parencite{maes2026}. This thesis does not follow this direction, for three reasons. First, practitioner reports describe agents that complete roughly 70\% of a task quickly but struggle with the remaining integration and edge cases, often ending in repeated fix loops \parencite{osmani2024, rezun2026}. Second, studies of coding agents report constraint violations, unintended changes to the environment and false claims that a task is complete \parencite{alhasan2026, uddin2026}. Third, without human involvement, no one in the organisation understands the application well enough to take responsibility for it, maintain it or hand it over \parencite{ginac2026, mitchell2026}. For applications used in productive environments this last point is decisive. Accountability must stay with a person like the citizen developer, while technical accountability is delegated to specialists.

The same reasoning applies to how the agents themselves are coordinated. In Agile V, a coordinating agent assigns work to the specialised agents and tells them which files to read, so that each starts with a fresh context \parencite{koch2026agile}. This thesis deliberately limits that role. The Planning Agent plans the change, keeps plan.md up to date and explains progress, decisions and problems to the citizen developer in non-technical language. However, it does not start or instruct the Build, Test Designer, Verification or Auditing Agents. There are four reasons for this. First, transparency: if agents manage other agents, several layers of abstraction separate the citizen developer from the work, which makes it harder for them to see what is happening and where the change stands. Second, independence: an agent that sees the code and also briefs the Test Designer could pass on implementation details. This would weaken the separation between building and testing, which replaces the code review that citizen developers cannot carry out. Third, traceability: when a person starts each step and the result is recorded in the shared files, every decision can be linked to a responsible person and to evidence. Fourth, collaboration: the plan becomes the single place where the citizen developer follows and steers the work. The approved specification stays stable, so plan.md is the only document that changes continuously during the build. Every agent reports its results there in short, plain language: completed tasks, test results, audit findings, decisions and open questions. The citizen developer therefore does not need to follow several chat sessions, remember what each agent was told or rely on an agent's own claim that a task is complete. Such claims are not always reliable \parencite{alhasan2026}. Instead, they read one document, keep it correct and react to its open questions. Each entry links to the underlying test or audit report, so progress can be checked against evidence rather than taken on trust. Because the plan is kept up to date at every stopping point, the work can also be resumed in a new session, or handed over to IT or other specialists, from this document alone \parencite{friel2025}. The plan thus serves as a shared reference point not only between the citizen developer and the agents, but also between the citizen developer and the experts who support them.

Separate contexts remain important, because model performance tends to decline as the input grows longer and mixes unrelated information \parencite{hong2025context}. The framework achieves this through a fresh session for each role rather than through an orchestrating agent. The Planning Agent may still use short-lived sub-agents for read-only tasks, such as summarising code or answering a question from the citizen developer. This keeps its own planning context free of technical detail \parencite{anthropic2025context}. These sub-agents do not change any files. The trade-off is that the citizen developer has to carry out more steps manually. The ADR cycles evaluate whether this extra complexity is acceptable (Section~TBD).


\subparagraph{Right Side: Verifying and Accepting the Change}
\label{sec:testing-verification}

Testing decides whether AI-generated work is accepted. Professional developers usually check code in two ways: they run tests and
they review the code itself. Citizen developers cannot read the code, so
additional tests and automated checks take the place of this code review. A change is
accepted because the evidence shows that it meets its specification, not
because it looks plausible \parencite{koch2026agentic}. This part deals only
with the \emph{independent} tests of a change: the tests that the Test
Designer Agent writes and the Verification Agent runs after the Build Agent
has finished all tasks. It does not cover the small self-checks that the
Build Agent runs within SCOPE-V while it builds; these are described in
Section~\ref{sec:build-self-checks} and do not count as evidence for
acceptance. Testing of the whole application follows later, in stage~4
(Section~\ref{sec:test-application}).

A separate Verification Agent, which corresponds to
the Red Team Verifier in Agile~V \parencite{koch2026agile} \footnote{In Agile~V, \enquote{red team} refers to independent
verification: the Red Team Verifier runs tests that were written without
access to the code and reports which requirements pass or fail
\parencite{koch2026agile}. It does not mean security red teaming, in which
the behaviour of an attacker is simulated. Adversarial security testing is
treated separately in this framework
(Sections~\ref{sec:security-review-agent}
and~\ref{sec:adversarial-security-tests}).}, runs the
independently written tests on the combined software. This differs from the
Verify step of SCOPE-V. There, the Build Agent checks its own work; here, the
Verification Agent tests the result independently. Tests on single tasks help
find errors in individual changes. 

 The Test Designer Agent writes its tests once
per change, from the approved feature specification. It works in parallel with the Build Agent, so
testing does not delay the build. In Agile~V, this separation prevents tests
that only confirm what the code happens to do instead of what it should do
\parencite{koch2026agile}. Table~\ref{tab:change-tests} lists the tests used, which the citizen developer can explicitly ask for in the Test Designer Agent prompt.
Besides the agent's tests, the automated pipeline runs the scans defined by
IT. The automated pipeline is a set of checks that IT configures once and
that runs automatically whenever a new version of the code is saved. The
scans are included here because they are part of the evidence at
Checkpoint~B.

Security is tested in two ways at this level. The
first way checks the code against general rules that IT defines once in the
system specification and in the pipeline. \emph{Static analysis} reads the
code without running it and looks for known unsafe patterns. \emph{Secret
scanning} searches for passwords or access keys written into the code. A
\emph{dependency scan} checks whether the external libraries used by the
application have known security problems. Agentic Agile-V lists such static
and security checks as required evidence for production changes
\parencite{koch2026agentic}. IT bases these rules on an established catalogue of common weaknesses in
web applications, such as the OWASP Top~10 \parencite{TODO-owasptop10}.
\textcite{sapkota2025} describe automated security auditing against this
catalogue as a typical task for coding agents: an agent runs static
analysis tools, applies fixes and writes a security report. This framework
uses the catalogue as a checklist for the rules and scans, but it does not
let one agent both find and fix weaknesses, because this would combine
checking and building in one role. Findings are returned to the Build
Agent like any other failed test. The example in \textcite{sapkota2025} is
an illustrative use case in a review, not an evaluated one.

The Build Agent runs quick versions of these
scans during its tasks (Section~\ref{sec:build-self-checks}); here, the
pipeline runs them in full for the whole change. The second way covers
security behaviour that depends on the feature, such as which user role may
approve a leave request. This behaviour is part of the feature specification,
so the Test Designer Agent turns it into acceptance and negative tests, for
example: \enquote{Given a user with the role employee, when this user opens
the approval page, then access is denied.}


\textbf{Protection against invented and unsafe libraries.} A third risk
concerns the external libraries that an agent adds to the application.
Language models sometimes recommend software packages that do not exist.
\textcite{TODO-huang2026package} collected 107 such invented package names
from eight models and registered them in the public npm registry. Over six
months, about a third of the installations were attributed to developers
rather than to automated scripts, and a quarter of the names differed from
an existing package by at most one character. In their setting, giving the
models web search did not solve the problem but made the same invented
names appear more often \parencite{TODO-huang2026package}. An attacker who
registers such a name can deliver harmful code to anyone who follows the
recommendation. A dependency scan may not detect this, because it mainly
reports known weaknesses of packages that already have a security record.
\textcite{TODO-huang2026package} therefore propose NymGuard, an agent that
checks recommended names against the registry, registers invented names
in advance and publishes warnings. NymGuard protects the public registry
and is not designed for use inside a company. The authors also note that
checking whether a name exists is not sufficient on its own, because an
attacker may already have registered it \parencite{TODO-huang2026package}.
This framework therefore adopts the underlying idea in a simpler form. IT
keeps a list of approved libraries in the system specification, and the
pipeline blocks any change that adds a library outside this list. A
request for a new library goes to IT for review. This extends the
Constrain step of SCOPE-V, which allows new external libraries only with a
reason, and follows the practitioner advice to use only well-established
libraries and to audit every dependency \parencite{TODO-koul2026vibesec}.
The allow-list is a design choice of this thesis; its effort for IT and
its effect on citizen developers have not been evaluated.
 
 
\textbf{Order of the tests.} After the Build Agent has completed all tasks,
the Verification Agent runs the independent tests in four steps, from narrow
to wide and from fast to slow, so that a failure can be traced to its cause:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Static analysis and security scans.} They are fast and do
    not need a running application. Clear rule violations are stopped before
    more expensive tests start.
    \item \textbf{Acceptance, contract, negative and boundary tests.} They
    check whether the new behaviour matches the specification. A failure
    here points to the change itself.
    \item \textbf{Integration tests.} They check whether the change works
    together with neighbouring parts.
    \item \textbf{Full regression suite.} It checks whether earlier functions
    still work. It runs last because it covers the most ground; a failure
    here shows that the change has side effects on other features.
\end{enumerate}

This order follows the guidance of Agentic Agile-V to run continuous
integration, static analysis and security checks before a change is merged
\parencite{koch2026agentic}. The exact sequence is a design choice of this
thesis.

\textbf{Repairs and protection of the tests.} If tests fail, the error
reports are sent back to the Build Agent, which fixes the code and submits it
again for testing. This feedback loop follows Agile~V, where the Build Agent
fixed the issues found by the verifier in a rework round
\parencite{koch2026agile}. It also follows the contract-based feedback loop of
VibeContract \parencite{wang2026vibecontract}. To avoid endless repair
attempts, the number of automatic repair rounds is limited. As one example,
\textcite{TODO-tddgovernance} allow three repair attempts and stop earlier when
the same failure repeats or a repair changes nothing. If the change still
fails after this limit, it is recorded in the plan as an unresolved finding
and passed to the citizen developer, who can research the problem to solve it
or pass it to an expert. The exact limit is a design choice of the
organisation. The tests themselves are also protected. Coding agents tend to
optimise for passing checks rather than for working software; they may change
test conditions or write extra scripts that force a test to pass
\parencite{TODO-kitchenloop}. The Build Agent may therefore not change the
independent tests. Every change to a test file is visible in version control
and requires approval by the IT reviewer. This rule is a design choice of
this thesis.


\textbf{Automated review of the code.}
\label{sec:security-review-agent}
The independent tests check what the specification describes. They do not
read the code, so they miss problems that no requirement mentions, such as
a password in a configuration file, an unsafe default setting or code that
is hard to maintain. Professional teams find such problems in code review,
which citizen developers cannot carry out. The framework therefore adds a
Review Agent that reads the change as a human reviewer would.
\textcite{TODO-kumar2025intuition} describe such a system in a company with
about 300 engineers: for every pull request, several agents review the
change in parallel, each with its own focus, such as security, error
detection, maintainability, performance and business logic. Their comments
are combined, duplicates are removed and each comment names the agent that
wrote it. Over one year, the time needed to review and close a pull
request fell by 31.8\%, and most engineers wanted to keep the review
agents. Human reviewers, however, still examined both the code and the
agents' comments before a change was merged, and a feature that accepted
AI suggestions automatically caused quality problems and was switched off
\parencite{TODO-kumar2025intuition}. The study measured productivity and
satisfaction, not how many security problems the agents found or missed,
and its participants were professional engineers, not citizen developers.
\textcite{waseem2025} likewise report that some teams use AI reviewers in
their pipelines, and recommend treating AI-generated code as a first draft
that must pass strict automated gates. In this framework, the Review Agent
is therefore advisory. The pipeline starts it, not the citizen developer;
it does not change code; and its comments are added to the evidence
bundle. At Checkpoint~B, they help the IT reviewer decide where to look
more closely, but they do not replace the technical approval. This role is
a design choice of this thesis.
 

\textbf{Responsibilities for the tests.} The citizen developer manages the
independent testing: they start the Test Designer and Verification Agents
with reviewed prompts and follow the results in \texttt{plan.md}. IT defines
the security and quality rules, configures the pipeline and the scanning
tools and judges their findings.

\textbf{Evidence bundle.} An Auditing Agent, which corresponds to the
Compliance Auditor in Agile~V, then collects all evidence in one package.
This package contains a traceability matrix, a table that links each
requirement to the code and the tests that checked it
\parencite{koch2026agile}. It also contains the test results, the results of
automated code analysis and security scans, important decisions and
unresolved findings, as well as known remaining risks and, for production
changes, a plan for undoing the change \parencite{koch2026agentic}. The
Auditing Agent also adds a short plain-language summary of this evidence to
\texttt{plan.md}, so that the citizen developer can follow the result in the
same document they use throughout the change.

\textbf{Checkpoint~B.} At Checkpoint~B, the change is accepted in two steps.
First, the citizen developer decides whether the change meets the business
need. This decision is based on the evidence package, not on reading the
code. Second, a technical expert approves the change. Only throwaway
prototypes are exempt from this technical approval. The risk class of a
change does not decide whether a technical review takes place, but how deep
it goes and which evidence it requires. If no expert is available, the change
remains restricted or on hold and is not treated as verified.

This follows both Agile~V sources, which keep technical approval in the loop.
In Agile~V, the responsible engineer holds final approval authority at the
second human gate \parencite{koch2026agile}. How strict the approval is
depends on the risk class of the change (Table~\ref{tab:change-classes}).

Changes in the prototype stage correspond to risk class R0. Most changes made by
citizen developers are expected to fall under R2 and therefore require
mandatory approval by a technical reviewer.

The framework does not remove this review but changes its form. Instead of
reading every line of code, the expert checks whether the evidence package
shows that the change meets its specification and looks more closely only
where the evidence is weak. \textcite{diaz2026} describe this shift as
evidence-backed acceptance and argue that it makes review faster and more
informative. The depth of the review is set explicitly for each type of
change and can be revised as experience grows \parencite{diaz2026}. The split
between business acceptance by the citizen developer and technical approval
by the expert is a design choice of this thesis. It aims to keep technical
control while reducing the effort that each change demands from scarce
experts.

\textbf{Return paths and release.} Errors in the code send the work back to
the task cycle. Major changes to requirements or constraints send it back to
Checkpoint~A. For readability, the figure leaves out these return paths. An
accepted change may be merged into the main code according to the repository
rules, but acceptance does not mean release to production. Instead, the
evidence from successive V-cycles feeds into the pilot and release decisions
of the lifecycle, together with checks of the whole application and
arrangements for operation. The same cycle is used again for changes during
the pilot and in later operation.

\textbf{Limits of testing a change.} Independent tests of a change have four
limits. First, all of them derive from the same feature specification. If a
requirement is wrong or missing, the tests confirm the mistake instead of
finding it. Second, other systems are often replaced by mocks, which may hide
problems with the real connections. Third, the tests focus on one change, not
on complete user tasks that cross several features. Fourth, passing tests
show that the specified requirements are covered, not that the software is
complete or free of errors. The Agile~V case study, for example, reported a
complete pass rate at the requirement level but did not use mutation testing,
branch coverage or fuzz testing \parencite{koch2026agile}. The Kitchen Loop
reaches a similar conclusion: tests of single functions and interfaces are
necessary but not sufficient, because they do not exercise the real execution
environment \parencite{TODO-kitchenloop}. Checkpoint~B therefore accepts a
change, but it does not show that the application is ready for users. This is

\subparagraph{Gate G3}

\paragraph{Stage 4: Test Application}
\label{sec:test-application}

After gate~G3, all planned changes have been accepted one by one. Stage~4
checks whether the application as a whole is ready for real users. It answers
three questions. Does the updated application still work as a whole? Do its
parts work together under real conditions? Are further changes needed before
users get access? The answers form the basis for the decision at gate~G4.

\textbf{Why a separate stage is needed.} Accepted changes do not guarantee a
working application. In the Kitchen Loop, an agent delivered a feature that
passed 38 unit tests and all style checks, yet it was completely broken,
because nobody had started the service and used it as a user would
\parencite{TODO-kitchenloop}. \textcite{TODO-tddev} argue that the correctness
of a web application cannot be judged from its source files or test output
alone; the application must be deployed and used through a browser. In their
benchmark experiments, testing the deployed application through a browser
improved results by 34 to 48 percentage points compared with no such testing
\parencite{TODO-tddev}. A second reason lies in this framework itself: all
tests in stage~3 are derived from specifications written by the citizen
developer (Section~\ref{sec:testing-verification}). Stage~4 adds two further
sources of truth: the users, who judge whether the application supports their
work, and IT, which judges the hidden qualities. MeRGE likewise treats quality
assurance as a phase of its own and found that human testing remained
unavoidable and that domain experts were indispensable \parencite{fetzer2026}.
This finding comes from systems that contain GenAI and is transferred here
with care.

\textbf{Responsibility depends on risk.} In stage~3, the citizen developer
manages most of the Agile~V cycle. In stage~4, responsibility for the
technical tests moves to IT, depending on the risk tier of the application, which was set provisionally
at G1, confirmed at G2 and reviewed at later gates. This shift reflects the different kind of evidence
now required. Citizen developers can judge whether software looks and behaves
as intended, but not whether it is secure or reliable
\parencite{kobiella2026}. For low-risk applications, IT provides a standard
test kit that the citizen developer can run and IT reviews. For higher risk
tiers, IT takes ownership of the technical tests. The citizen developer and
the business owner remain responsible for the business tests in every tier.
Table~\ref{tab:g4-responsibility} shows this distribution. R0 applications do
not enter stage~4, because they remain prototypes.

\textbf{Preparing the tests early.} Several test inputs are created before
stage~4 begins. At G2, the citizen developer writes the main user journeys and
short test cards together with users and the business owner. A test card is a
step-by-step task with an expected result, for example: \enquote{Submit a
leave request for three days. Expected result: the request appears for the
manager and the balance shows three days less.} The cards are based on the
prototype walkthroughs and on the success criteria agreed at G1. They are
written before the code exists and are not given to the Build Agent. This
follows the information wall of the Kitchen Loop, in which the evaluator
receives only the test card and no code or implementation context. The
Kitchen Loop also requires each card to describe only behaviour that users can
see and to include at least one step in which a wrong input is rejected
\parencite{TODO-kitchenloop}. Unlike in the Kitchen Loop, where the
implementing agent writes the cards, here the users write them, because they
know best what the application must do. The citizen developer also prepares a
role matrix that states which role may see and do what. During stage~3, the
regression suite grows with every accepted change. IT provides a reusable test
kit: a staging environment, an automated pipeline, scanning rules, templates
for role tests and a rollback procedure. Because the kit is built once and
reused for every application, expert effort per application is expected to
decrease. When stage~4 starts, the application is frozen as a release
candidate, a fixed and labelled version in version control. During testing,
only fixes are made, no new features.

\textbf{Agents in this stage.} The agents from stage~3 keep their roles but
work on the whole application. The Test Designer Agent turns the user journeys
and test cards into executable browser tests, again without access to the
code. The Verification Agent runs the regression suite and the browser tests
on the staging environment. A browser test opens the application and clicks
and types like a user. When a test fails, the agent writes a short report that
states what was attempted, where it failed and what was visible on the
screen. This follows the approach of TDDev, which converts requirements into
acceptance tests, runs them in the browser and translates failures into
structured reports \parencite{TODO-tddev}. Such test agents are not fully
reliable: in TDDev, the testing agent's verdict was correct in 87.5\% of
cases, with errors only in the form of missed passes \parencite{TODO-tddev}.
These results come from benchmark tasks and a user study with three
professional developers, not with citizen developers. Screenshots are
therefore kept as evidence, and failures of critical journeys are checked by a
person.


\textbf{Adversarial security tests.}
\label{sec:adversarial-security-tests}
For R2 and R3 applications, a security test agent additionally attacks the
running application on staging. Unlike the Verification Agent, it does not
check whether the specification is met but tries to break the application.
It acts from the perspective of each role and attempts forbidden actions,
for example opening the leave data of another team through a direct link,
changing a request that belongs to someone else or sending manipulated
input to a form. Several practitioner sources describe this idea. In an
experience report, \textcite{waseem2025} scanned seven early vibe-coded
applications with an agentic security scanner and found 970 security
issues, most of them rated as highly severe; one co-author proposes
\enquote{white-hat hacker agents} that carry out controlled attacks during
development and acceptance. BCG Platinion lists dedicated red-team agents
that probe adversarial edge cases as one layer of verification in an
agentic software factory \parencite{bcgplatinion2026agentic}. A
Thoughtworks team that scaled a vibe-coded prototype asked the AI to act as
an attacker and test what it had built, and reported that this regularly
revealed weaknesses in permissions and data exposure
\parencite{TODO-koul2026vibesec}. None of these sources reports how many
real weaknesses such agents find or miss. \textcite{waseem2025} therefore
recommend using such agents as an additional defence and checking their
findings regularly through manual penetration tests. The framework follows
this advice: the agent works only on staging with test data, its findings
go to IT security, and for R3 applications a penetration test by IT
security remains mandatory. Confirmed findings become regression tests
and, if they reveal a recurring pattern, a rule in the system
specification, so that red-team findings improve the rules for later
changes \parencite{bcgplatinion2026agentic}. Finally, the Auditing Agent
compiles the G4 evidence report and adds a plain-language summary to
\texttt{plan.md}. As in stage~3, no agent approves anything
\parencite{alhasan2026}.

The order follows three rules. First, automated tests come before human tests.
Users' and experts' time is scarce, so they should only test a version that
already works technically. Second, cheap tests come before expensive ones. If
the smoke test fails, there is no point in running the other tests. Third, the
tests move from the inside to the outside. They first check whether earlier
functions still work (step~2), then whether the parts connect to the real
systems (step~3), then whether complete tasks succeed (step~4) and finally
whether the application is secure, lawful and usable for real work
(steps~5--8). The Kitchen Loop describes a similar layering, in which tests of
real execution and complete user journeys give the highest level of trust
\parencite{TODO-kitchenloop}. Two choices adapt its principles to citizen
development. User acceptance is placed late, because users judge the
application best when it is technically stable. And the \enquote{weakest
evaluator} principle of the Kitchen Loop, in which a deliberately weak model
executes the test cards \parencite{TODO-kitchenloop}, is translated into a
human rule: test cards are also carried out by a colleague who has not seen
the application before. If they can follow the card, real users can too.

After a fix, the smoke test, the full regression suite and all failed or
affected tests are repeated. User acceptance is only repeated if the fix
changes behaviour that users can see. Returns from this stage to development
are expected and not a sign of failure: in MeRGE, the most frequent
transitions occurred between development and quality assurance
\parencite{fetzer2026}.

\textbf{Gate G4.} At G4, the decision-makers check whether:

\begin{itemize}
    \item all blocking criteria in Table~\ref{tab:g4-criteria} are met;
    \item every requirement is linked to a passed test;
    \item the business owner has accepted the user acceptance results;
    \item the security and data protection checks required for the risk tier
    are completed and accepted;
    \item rollback, monitoring, support and an operations owner are in place;
    \item open minor findings are documented with an owner and a deadline.
\end{itemize}

If all criteria are met, the application moves to the pilot in stage~5 (Go).
Otherwise, the gate decides to revise, restrict, hand over or stop. A
restriction may, for example, allow a pilot only with fewer roles or without
real personal data. A handover is appropriate when the tests show that the
application exceeds what citizen development can safely deliver, for example
because integrations with enterprise systems fail repeatedly. In such cases,
research on low-code development recommends that IT co-develops or leads
\parencite{viljoen2024}.

\textbf{Testing later updates.} Changes made during the pilot and operation
pass through the Agile~V cycle of stage~3 and then through a reduced version of stage~4. The
automated tests (smoke test, full regression suite and scans) always run in
full, because they are cheap. The human tests (end-to-end checks by the
citizen developer, role reviews by IT and user acceptance) are repeated only
for the parts of the application affected by the change. The rule is simple:
automated tests always check everything; people check what has changed.

The structure of this stage, the shift of responsibility to IT by risk tier,
the early test cards written by users and the order of the tests are design
choices of this thesis. They build on practitioner reports and on studies with
professional developers. Whether they work for citizen developers is evaluated
through eADR. The evaluation examines how many errors stage~4 finds that passed
Checkpoint~B, how many of them stem from the specification, whether users can
write usable test cards and whether the effort for IT remains manageable.

\paragraph{Stage 5: Pilot}

\paragraph{Stage 6: Operate and Reassess}

\textbf{Monitoring new vulnerabilities.} An application that passed all
checks at release can become insecure later, when a weakness is found in
one of its libraries, in the platform or in the AI coding tools
themselves. During operation, the framework therefore includes a security
intelligence feed. \textcite{TODO-koul2026vibesec} describe such a feed at
Thoughtworks: an automated workflow monitors the languages, cloud platforms
and AI coding tools that a team uses and delivers a daily digest of new
vulnerabilities and security advisories, so that the team learns of a
problem on the day it is published. In this framework, IT runs the feed
centrally for all citizen-developed applications and matches each advisory
with the libraries recorded for each application. The operations owner is
informed when an application is affected. The same authors envision agents
that create work items and prepare patches for known weaknesses for human
review, but they present this as a future step, not as current practice
\parencite{TODO-koul2026vibesec}. The framework allows such an agent to
prepare a fix, but the fix is treated like any other change: it passes
through the Agile~V cycle of stage~3 and the reduced version of stage~4,
including technical approval at Checkpoint~B
(Section~\ref{sec:test-application}). Urgent fixes may follow a shortened
path, but they never skip the automated tests or the technical approval.
Central monitoring and the shortened path are design choices of this
thesis. They aim to spare each citizen developer from following security
news alone.

A primary limitation of this study is its reliance on a single researcher to both apply the framework and interpret the evaluation outcomes. Because the findings depend heavily on a single-researcher perspective and self-assessments, there is a risk of subjective bias where alternative interpretations or approaches might be missed. Due to the practical resource constraints inherent in a single-author master's thesis, independent evaluations involving multiple reviewers were not feasible. However, this single-researcher approach is considered acceptable within the scope of a pioneering exploratory study in a rapidly evolving technological field. To strengthen objectivity and validate these initial findings, future research should focus on independent replications of the framework, utilizing multiple independent reviewers to minimize self-assessment bias.
